Political feasibility is the most rate-limiting variable in this wiki. The post-2008 period has produced abundant evidence that the public *wants* less inequality (consistent majorities across nearly all advanced democracies), but durable enactment has been rare. The gap between public opinion and policy outcomes is itself one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the wealth-to-power conversion described in [Section 1](/my-wiki/current-state/power-concentration-mechanisms/).

A useful frame is to separate interventions into three feasibility tiers. **Tier 1 (winnable now)** includes interventions with elite buy-in, technocratic legitimacy, or international momentum: beneficial-ownership transparency, the OECD Pillar Two minimum, and antitrust enforcement at agencies that already have authority. **Tier 2 (medium-horizon)** includes interventions that require legislative action and political capital but have proven coalitions: sectoral bargaining (in some European contexts), capital-gains reform, and public campaign financing. **Tier 3 (long-horizon or transformational)** includes interventions that require constitutional change, deep coalition-building, or shifts in elite consensus: [wealth taxes](/my-wiki/interventions/progressive-taxation/), billionaire minimum taxes, supreme court reform, and major [antitrust breakups](/my-wiki/interventions/antitrust-and-competition/).

Sequencing matters: Tier 1 wins build organizational capacity and political constituencies that make Tier 2 possible; Tier 2 wins build the political infrastructure for Tier 3. The historical analog is the Progressive Era (1900–1916), when transparency reforms (food and drug labeling, corporate disclosure) preceded the income tax (1913) and antitrust expansion (1914), each step building on the prior one.

## The opinion-policy gap

The political-feasibility puzzle starts with a striking empirical pattern: **public majorities in essentially every advanced democracy support policies more redistributive than what their governments actually enact**, often by large margins. This is the "opinion-policy gap" first systematically documented by Page and Bartels and now extensively studied across countries.

[Page, Bartels & Seawright (2013, *Perspectives on Politics*, "Democracy and the Policy Preferences of Wealthy Americans")](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/jnd260/cab/CAB2012%20-%20Page1.pdf) found that:

- **59% of the U.S. public said they would be willing to pay more taxes** for tax-financed public services; only 41% of wealthy Americans agreed.
- **61% of the public favored tax-financed national health insurance**; only 32% of the wealthy did.
- The wealthy systematically favored less government spending, less regulation, and less progressive taxation than the median voter — but enacted policy consistently aligned with the wealthy preference.

[Bartels (*Unequal Democracy*, 2008/2016)](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691172842/unequal-democracy) extended this with a finding worth taking seriously on its own terms: Americans supported the Bush-era tax cuts not because they were indifferent to inequality but because they **largely failed to connect inequality and tax policy** in their reasoning. Three out of four poll respondents agreed that income differences had increased and that this was a bad thing — but the same respondents did not see the Bush tax cuts as contributing to inequality. The mechanism is informational and framing-related, not preference-based.

The implications for political feasibility are paradoxical. The substantive support for redistribution is *already there* in the public; what is missing is the political infrastructure to translate it into enacted policy. The interventions in this wiki are not pushing against a hostile public — they are pushing against an organized minority that has captured a disproportionate share of agenda-setting and policy-implementation capacity. The most-cited statistical demonstration is [Gilens & Page (2014, *Perspectives on Politics*, "Testing Theories of American Politics")](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/abs/testing-theories-of-american-politics-elites-interest-groups-and-average-citizens/62327F513959D0A304D4893B382B992B), which found that economic elites and business-interest groups have substantial independent influence on U.S. policy outcomes while average citizens have "little or no independent influence." The opinion-policy gap is the operational space in which the [four-pillar strategy](/my-wiki/synthesis/core-strategy/) operates.

What closes the gap, when it does close, is some combination of:

1. **Organized constituencies** that translate latent public support into mobilized political pressure (unions, advocacy organizations, social movements).
2. **Informational shifts** that connect specific policies to inequality outcomes in voters' reasoning (Bartels-style framing changes).
3. **Crisis windows** that disrupt elite policy capture and create unusual political openings (1929, 1945, 2008, 2020).
4. **Elite defection** that reduces the credibility of elite opposition and shifts the perceived legitimacy of redistribution (Patriotic Millionaires, post-2008 banker reformers).

Each of these is discussed below.

## The Progressive Era as a sequencing model

The most useful historical analog to the strategy in this wiki is the [U.S. Progressive Era (~1900–1916)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Era), which produced the largest peacetime regulatory expansion in U.S. history through a clear-eyed sequencing strategy that built each reform on the institutional and political infrastructure of the previous one.

The basic sequence:

| Period       | Reforms enacted                                                | Pillar(s)                       |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| 1901–1909 (TR) | Sherman Act enforcement; ICC strengthened; Hepburn Act; Elkins Act; Pure Food and Drug Act | Defend the rules               |
| 1909–1913 (Taft) | 16th Amendment ratified; Postal Savings; Mann-Elkins Act     | Tax the top                    |
| 1913–1916 (Wilson) | Underwood Tariff (income tax); Federal Reserve Act; Federal Trade Commission Act; Clayton Antitrust Act | All four pillars              |

Sources: dates from the [U.S. Progressive Era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_Era) record; the 16th Amendment was ratified in February 1913 and the income tax enacted in the Underwood Tariff later that year.

Notice the sequence. The first wave (Roosevelt) was largely *transparency and procedural* — the Pure Food and Drug Act, ICC inspection authority, antitrust enforcement against the most egregious cases. The second wave (Taft) shifted toward *substantive redistribution* (the constitutional amendment authorizing the federal income tax). The third wave (Wilson) consolidated the architecture (Federal Reserve, FTC, Clayton Antitrust) and built it into permanent institutions.

The political coalition that produced this sequence was unusual: it bridged Northern progressives, Southern populists, Western farm interests, Eastern intellectuals, and (selectively) the Republican Party. The bridges were possible because the early-stage interventions had broad-enough support to move incrementally before any single intervention had to bear the full weight of the redistributive agenda.

The lessons for the contemporary strategy:

1. **Sequencing matters.** The Progressive Era did not start with the income tax (the most ambitious intervention) — it started with anti-corruption, transparency, and procedural reforms that built political capacity for the income tax.
2. **Coalition-bridging is feasible** when early-stage interventions are framed in terms (anti-corruption, fairness, accountability) that have broader appeal than redistribution per se.
3. **Permanent institutions matter more than individual policies.** The Progressive Era's most durable contribution was the Federal Reserve, FTC, and federal income tax *as institutions* — these long outlasted any individual reform within them.
4. **Crisis acceleration.** The Progressive Era did not just unfold linearly; it accelerated sharply in response to specific events (Triangle Shirtwaist Fire 1911, *The Jungle* 1906, the Pujo Committee revelations 1912–13). Strategy under non-crisis conditions builds the capacity to act when crises arrive.

The contemporary parallel is roughly: Tier 1 interventions (transparency, agency-level antitrust, Pillar Two) are the early Progressive analogs; Tier 2 interventions (sectoral bargaining, capital gains, public financing) are the Wilson-era substantive consolidation; Tier 3 interventions (wealth taxes, billionaire minimums) are the constitutional-amendment scale of ambition.

## Country case studies

| Country     | Stage of pillar architecture                          | Binding constraint                       |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **Norway**  | Tier 3 mostly complete (SWF, wealth tax, codetermination, public investment) | Maintaining political durability against rising populist pressure |
| **Germany** | Tier 2 mature (sectoral bargaining, codetermination, predistribution); Tier 3 partial | Wealth-tax revival; coalition fragility   |
| **France**  | Tier 2 mature; some Tier 3 (recently lost ISF wealth tax) | Restoring lost Tier 3 wins                |
| **Sweden / Denmark** | Tier 2 mature; Tier 3 retreating (wealth-tax abolitions) | Reversing 2000s/2010s rollbacks       |
| **U.K.**    | Tier 1 partial (beneficial ownership advanced); Tier 2 weak | Building labor and predistribution infrastructure |
| **U.S.**    | Tier 1 partial (Pillar Two not implemented; [Corporate Transparency Act gutted](https://www.fincen.gov/boi) — a March 2025 FinCEN rule exempted all domestic companies); Tier 2 minimal | Cross-pillar building from a weak base   |
| **Brazil / India / South Africa** | Tier 1 building (transparency efforts active); Tier 2 minimal | State-capacity constraints; international coordination |

Sources: France's ISF wealth tax was abolished in 2018 (replaced by the narrower real-estate IFI); Germany's wealth tax lapsed after the Constitutional Court's 1995 valuation ruling; the Nordic abolitions and Norway's retained wealth tax are detailed in [Progressive Taxation](/my-wiki/interventions/progressive-taxation/), and the broader risk/tradeoff picture in [Risks and Tradeoffs](/my-wiki/synthesis/risks-and-tradeoffs/).

**Norway** is the cleanest example of a country that built the four-pillar architecture relatively completely and has maintained it across multiple decades. The main current challenge is durability under shifting politics rather than building new components.

**Germany** has unusually strong build-power and tax pillars but a relatively weak wealth-tax / billionaire-tax stance (the Constitutional Court's 1995 valuation ruling effectively ended the wealth tax). Recent SPD/Greens advocacy has put wealth-tax revival back on the agenda; the CDU has resisted.

**France** had a strong wealth-tax architecture (ISF) until its 2018 abolition under Macron — a substantial Tier 3 retreat. Recovery efforts are ongoing.

**The U.S.** sits at the bottom of the pillar-completeness ranking. The strategy here implicitly addresses the U.S. case more than others, because the institutional gaps are larger and the political-feasibility puzzles are more pronounced.

The cross-country lesson is that **the four-pillar architecture is achievable** — Norway demonstrates this — but it is achievable through *country-specific paths*, not a single template. The Norwegian path went through resource-revenue-funded universal welfare; the German path went through sectoral bargaining and codetermination; the French path went through statist progressive taxation. Other countries will need other paths suited to their institutional histories.

## Crises and what they unlock

The Scheidel thesis from [Historical Trajectory](/my-wiki/current-state/historical-trajectory/) — that only catastrophic violence produces large peaceful leveling — is overstated, but it is true that *crisis windows* substantially accelerate political possibility. Three U.S. cases illustrate the pattern:

**1929 → 1933–1938 (New Deal)**: The Great Depression discredited the Hoover-era policy regime and produced the most concentrated burst of redistributive policy in U.S. history. The [Wagner Act (1935)](https://www.nlrb.gov/about-nlrb/who-we-are/our-history/1935-passage-of-the-wagner-act), Social Security (1935), Glass-Steagall (1933), SEC (1934), and federal estate tax expansion (the 1935 Revenue Act) all came in a 5-year window that would have been unimaginable in 1928.

**1945 → 1948 (post-war settlement)**: Wartime mobilization produced policy infrastructure (price controls, capital controls, [top federal income-tax rates of 91–94%](https://taxpolicycenter.org/sites/default/files/legacy/taxfacts/content/PDF/toprate_historical.pdf) — 94% in 1944–45, 91% from 1946 to 1963 — and expansive labor coverage) that remained substantially in place for the entire post-war compression.

**2008 → 2010–2015 (Dodd-Frank, ACA)**: The financial crisis produced Dodd-Frank (2010), the Affordable Care Act (2010), and the structural foundation of the post-2010 financial-regulation architecture. The window was smaller and more contested than 1933, but it produced reforms that would have been impossible in 2007.

**2020 → 2021–? (pandemic + recession)**: The COVID-era response included substantial expansions of unemployment insurance, the expanded Child Tax Credit, the American Rescue Plan, and several state-level UBI pilots. Most of the immediate expansions sunset; some structural changes (state UBI infrastructure, broader Child Tax Credit advocacy) persist.

The pattern is that **crises do not produce reforms automatically; they widen political windows that pre-existing reform agendas can move through**. The New Deal was possible because Progressive-era reformers had spent two decades building the policy designs and political coalitions that were ready to move when the 1929 crisis hit. The reforms that *didn't* happen in those windows were typically the ones that lacked pre-built design and constituency.

The strategic implication is that **building reform-ready capacity during non-crisis periods is the highest-leverage long-horizon investment**. The interventions in this wiki should be thought of partly as substantive policies and partly as the design infrastructure for reforms that will be enacted during future crisis windows.

## Elite defection

The fourth lever for closing the opinion-policy gap is **elite defection** — wealthy individuals and businesses that publicly advocate for redistribution against their own narrow material interests. This works on two margins: it reduces the credibility of "elite opposition" as a coordinated bloc, and it provides cover for politicians who would otherwise face the "anti-business" framing that has been politically lethal for redistribution proposals since the 1980s.

The contemporary U.S. examples include:

- **[Patriotic Millionaires](https://patrioticmillionaires.org/about/)** (founded 2010 by Erica Payne, originally as "Patriotic Millionaires for Fiscal Strength"): a group of high-net-worth individuals advocating for higher taxes on themselves and reform of campaign finance and labor law. It now numbers around 175 members (as of 2024) and has been the most visible elite-defection vehicle on tax policy.
- **Resource Generation**: Organizes wealthy young inheritors around social-justice giving and policy advocacy; provides the "next generation" voice for inequality politics.
- **Solidaire Network**: Pools high-net-worth funding for movement organizations.
- **Individual signatories**: Warren Buffett's "Buffett Rule" advocacy; Ray Dalio's inequality writings; Marc Benioff's wealth-tax advocacy in California.

The historical analog is the 1930s "Class Traitor" wealthy progressives (FDR himself, Marriner Eccles, Henry Morgenthau Jr., Frances Perkins, the Rosenwald and Filene families) who provided the elite political legitimacy for the New Deal. Their participation reframed redistribution from "class warfare" to "patriotic obligation" and substantially eased the political path.

The mechanism is not that elite defectors swing votes directly — they don't — but that they alter the *coalitional landscape*. A redistribution proposal opposed by 100% of the wealthy is easier for centrist politicians to oppose than a proposal opposed by 80% of the wealthy with a vocal 20% in support. The defecting minority creates space for politicians who would otherwise face uniform elite hostility.

The limit of elite defection is that it cannot substitute for organized mass constituencies. The Patriotic Millionaires can provide cover for a wealth tax; only an organized labor movement or mass mobilization can produce the political pressure to enact it. Elite defection is *complementary* to the build-power pillar of the strategy — useful but not sufficient on its own.
