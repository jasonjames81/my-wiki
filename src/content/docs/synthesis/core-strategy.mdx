No single intervention in [Section 2](/interventions/progressive-taxation/) is sufficient on its own. Progressive taxation without financial transparency leaks through offshore wealth. Antitrust without labor power redistributes from one set of capital owners to another. Democratic reforms without redistributive policy leave the structural sources of concentration intact. The synthesis question is which combination is internally reinforcing — where the political and economic feedback loops compound rather than cancel.

This wiki's working answer is a four-pillar strategy:

1. **Make wealth visible** (financial regulation + global tax coordination) so that domestic policy levers can actually bind.
2. **Tax the top** (inheritance + capital gains + coordinated minimum taxes) to compress the distribution and fund the bottom pillars.
3. **Build power at the bottom** (labor reform + predistribution + employee ownership) to create a durable political constituency for the first two pillars.
4. **Defend the rules** (democratic reforms + antitrust enforcement) to prevent the recurrence of capture.

Each pillar reinforces the others. Tax revenue funds predistribution; predistribution builds the political base for further taxation; financial transparency expands the tax base; democratic reforms protect all three from rollback. Removing any single pillar produces a system that is structurally unstable on a 10–20 year horizon — which is roughly what happened to the post-war compression as labor power collapsed in the 1970s–80s.

## The four pillars

| Pillar           | Primary interventions                                                            | Function in the strategy            |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Make wealth visible | [Financial Regulation](/interventions/financial-regulation/), [Global Tax Coordination](/interventions/global-tax-coordination/) | Pre-condition for everything else |
| Tax the top      | [Progressive Taxation](/interventions/progressive-taxation/)                     | Compresses distribution, funds (3)  |
| Build power      | [Labor Power](/interventions/labor-power/), [Predistribution](/interventions/predistribution/) | Creates durable political base    |
| Defend the rules | [Democratic Reforms](/interventions/democratic-reforms/), [Antitrust](/interventions/antitrust-and-competition/) | Prevents capture and reversal     |

Sources: Four-pillar structure developed in [Hacker & Pierson (2010)](https://www.amazon.com/Winner-Take-All-Politics-Washington-Richer-Turned/dp/1416588701) and the complementarity argument in [Hacker (2011)](https://assets.website-files.com/64a8727f61fabfaa63b7b770/64aab85cf813ccbee57ce07f_hacker_pn.pdf). See also [Political Feasibility](/synthesis/political-feasibility/) for sequencing constraints.

## Why all four pillars are jointly necessary

The argument for the four-pillar structure is not that any single pillar lacks merit; each one is independently supported by substantial empirical evidence (Section 2). The argument is that **any subset of three pillars is structurally unstable on a 10–20 year horizon**, because the missing pillar permits the wealth-power feedback loop to reassemble through the gap.

Consider the failure modes of each three-pillar subset:

| Missing pillar         | Failure mode                                                                                    | Historical example          |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Make wealth visible    | Tax revenue from upper pillars leaks through offshore; political pressure for transparency builds | 1990s wealth-tax era; pre-CRS |
| Tax the top            | Universalism pillar lacks revenue base; predistribution becomes politically unaffordable; bottom-up pressure decays | Post-2008 austerity period |
| Build power            | Defensive pillars cannot resist quiet rollback by concentrated interests; reforms hollow over decades | Post-1980 U.S. labor decline + tax-cut cycles |
| Defend the rules       | Concentrated wealth captures regulators and reshapes the rules of the game; substantive policies become nominal | Antitrust 1980–2020; Citizens United |

Sources: Failure-mode examples drawn from [Hacker & Pierson (2010)](https://www.amazon.com/Winner-Take-All-Politics-Washington-Richer-Turned/dp/1416588701); European austerity column draws on [Historical Trajectory](/current-state/historical-trajectory/).

The 1980–2020 U.S. trajectory illustrates the third failure mode in detail. The post-war compression had built substantial labor power (union density >30%, peaking near 35% in the mid-1950s), substantial top-end taxation (top marginal rates 91–94% through 1963, then 70% through 1981), and meaningful antitrust enforcement (the AT&T era). But the *defensive* pillar — democratic reforms that would prevent concentrated wealth from capturing the policy-making process — was relatively weak, with weak campaign finance limits, no public financing, weak lobbying disclosure, and a Supreme Court increasingly receptive to corporate-speech arguments. Over four decades, concentrated wealth used its political access to systematically dismantle the labor and tax pillars without facing structural resistance from the defensive pillar.

The reverse pattern — pillars without the *power-building* foundation — is illustrated by the early-2010s European experience. Several European countries had strong democratic-reform regimes (campaign finance limits, lobbying disclosure, technocratic regulators) and substantial top-end taxation, but had allowed labor-power and predistribution to weaken substantially since 1990. The post-2008 austerity programs — which fell disproportionately on labor and the bottom of the distribution — encountered limited political resistance because the constituency for resistance had been hollowed out. Top-end taxation continued, but on a base that was no longer growing relative to the bottom.

The constructive claim is that the **four pillars are *complements* in the economic sense — the marginal effect of each pillar increases when the others are also present**. Tax revenue funded by progressive taxation finances predistribution programs; predistribution programs build durable political constituencies for further taxation; financial transparency makes the tax base wider and harder to avoid; democratic reforms make the entire architecture difficult to roll back. Removing any one pillar reduces the productivity of the others.

## The post-war compression as a natural experiment

The empirical analog of the four-pillar argument is the post-war compression itself. Between 1940 and 1950, U.S. wage inequality collapsed dramatically — [Goldin and Margo's 1992 *QJE* paper](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/107/1/1/1925779) documented the 90/10 log-wage differential for men falling from 1.414 to 1.060 in a single decade. The compression coincided with the simultaneous adoption of all four pillars in roughly recognizable form:

- **Make wealth visible**: Wartime financial controls, capital controls, mandatory reporting on financial transactions.
- **Tax the top**: Top marginal income tax rates of [94% (1944–45), 91% (1946–63)](https://taxpolicycenter.org/sites/default/files/legacy/taxfacts/content/PDF/toprate_historical.pdf); estate tax with broad base; corporate tax at 50%+; wartime excess profits taxation.
- **Build power**: [Wagner Act 1935](https://www.nlrb.gov/about-nlrb/who-we-are/our-history/1935-passage-of-the-wagner-act) + War Labor Board enforcement; union density rising to ~35% of the workforce by 1945–54; federal minimum wage; G.I. Bill universal asset distribution.
- **Defend the rules**: New Deal regulatory agencies (SEC, NLRB, FTC); Glass-Steagall; antitrust under Thurman Arnold; expanded federal oversight of finance.

The compression *persisted* for roughly 30 years (1948–1978) — a sustained period of low and falling inequality. The persistence is the important fact. Many policy regimes produce short-term compression that erodes within a decade; the post-war compression was robust to multiple presidential transitions, the Cold War, decolonization, and major macroeconomic shocks. Its durability is the empirical case for the four-pillar architecture.

The unwinding from 1978 onward is equally instructive. The reversals were not simultaneous but cumulative, and they reinforced each other (see [Historical Trajectory](/current-state/historical-trajectory/)). Top tax rates were cut first (1978–1986), enabling top-end accumulation that funded political pressure for further cuts. Antitrust enforcement was relaxed (1980 onward), enabling firm consolidation that produced larger lobbying capacity. Labor-power institutions were weakened (Reagan's PATCO confrontation 1981; PATCO precedent then applied across the private sector). Financial regulation was dismantled (1980s S&L deregulation; 1999 Glass-Steagall repeal; 2000 CFMA). Each reversal made the next one easier.

The lesson is that **the four-pillar architecture is built and dismantled as a system, not as individual policies**. A strategy of restoring one pillar at a time — say, raising top tax rates while leaving the labor and antitrust regimes weak — would face the same instability that the post-war architecture would have faced if it had been built one piece at a time across decades.

The most important academic statement of this thesis is [Hacker & Pierson's *Winner-Take-All Politics* (2010)](https://www.amazon.com/Winner-Take-All-Politics-Washington-Richer-Turned/dp/1416588701), which documents the political-economy mechanics of the post-1978 unwinding in detail and frames it as the work of organized political forces (not "natural" market processes). Their concept of **policy drift** — the failure to update policies as economic conditions change, with predictably regressive consequences — is the operational definition of the missing-pillar failure mode.

## Counter-examples and dissenting views

The four-pillar argument has serious critics. Three lines of objection are worth engaging directly.

**The Hayek/Mises capital-strike critique.** The libertarian-conservative tradition (Hayek 1944, *Road to Serfdom*; Mises *Human Action* 1949) argues that aggressive redistribution and intervention will produce a *capital strike* — investment, entrepreneurship, and growth will collapse as risk-takers are deterred by the prospect of expropriation. The post-war compression, on this view, was bought at the cost of slower growth that eventually became unsustainable; the post-1980 reversal was the necessary correction.

The empirical record on this is mixed. Growth rates were *higher* in the post-war compression period (1948–1978) than in the post-1980 reversal in most advanced economies — a fact the libertarian tradition struggles to explain on its own terms. The capital-strike prediction has been falsified often enough (1990s tax increases under Clinton; 2010s wealth-tax revivals in Spain and Norway; multiple state-level minimum-wage increases) that it should be treated as a hypothesis rather than a settled fact. But it is genuinely a hypothesis, not a refuted claim — large enough redistributive shifts could in principle produce the predicted collapse, and the question is one of magnitude and design.

**The technocratic-redistribution critique.** A more centrist objection is that the right strategy is *not* the four-pillar architecture but a narrower technocratic approach: high-quality public education, well-targeted means-tested transfers, sensible regulation, modest progressive taxation. The "Nordic model lite" rather than the more politically ambitious project described here. The case for this view is that it is more politically tractable and that it has produced better outcomes than the U.S. without requiring the more radical institutional changes.

The response is empirical: the technocratic Nordic model has *also* been eroding, with rising inequality in Sweden, Denmark, and Finland over the past two decades. The argument is not that technocratic redistribution is wrong but that it is insufficient — it operates on the post-distribution margin without addressing the structural forces that generate pre-distribution inequality. Without the labor-power and antitrust pillars, even the Nordic technocracy is being slowly hollowed out by the same forces operating elsewhere.

**The predistribution-only critique.** From the left, [Hacker himself argues](https://assets.website-files.com/64a8727f61fabfaa63b7b770/64aab85cf813ccbee57ce07f_hacker_pn.pdf) that the strategy should focus more heavily on predistribution (institutional design of markets) and less on tax-and-transfer redistribution, because predistribution outcomes are more politically durable. The four-pillar framework here is broadly consistent with this argument but treats predistribution as one component (the build-power pillar) rather than the whole strategy. The risk of pure predistribution-without-redistribution is that it leaves accumulated stocks of wealth untouched, allowing the existing concentration to persist for generations even as new generation flows are equalized.

The honest position is that the four-pillar argument is *contested*, not settled. The case for it rests on (a) the historical pattern of the post-war compression and its unwinding, (b) the empirical evidence for each individual pillar (Section 2), and (c) the structural argument that the pillars reinforce each other in the same way the post-1980 reversals reinforced each other. Reasonable people working in this space disagree on the relative weight of each pillar and on the political feasibility of the integrated approach.

## How this maps to prioritization

The four-pillar argument does not imply that all four pillars must advance *simultaneously*. The empirical pattern is that **pillar restoration follows a sequence**, with the early-stage interventions building the political and institutional infrastructure for the later-stage ones.

[Prioritized Interventions](/synthesis/prioritized-interventions/) develops this in detail. The summary:

1. **Tier 1 (foundation)**: Beneficial-ownership transparency, [Pillar Two](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/global-minimum-tax.html) corporate minimum, agency-level antitrust enforcement. These are make-wealth-visible and defend-the-rules interventions that have elite buy-in or technocratic legitimacy and can advance without major legislative battles.
2. **Tier 2 (substance)**: Sectoral bargaining, capital-gains reform, public campaign financing, employee ownership scaling. These are tax-the-top and build-power interventions that require legislative action and meaningful political capital but have proven coalitions.
3. **Tier 3 (transformation)**: Wealth taxes, billionaire minimum tax, supreme court reform, structural breakups. These are the most ambitious interventions across all four pillars and require either mass mobilization, electoral realignment, or both.

Each tier builds the conditions for the next. Tier 1 successes produce revenue and political constituencies that make Tier 2 conceivable; Tier 2 successes build the labor and predistribution infrastructure that makes Tier 3 politically possible.

The four pillars are not in tension with this sequencing. Each tier advances the four pillars in roughly equal measure — the early-stage interventions are spread across the pillars, not concentrated in one — so the system stays balanced even as it grows in ambition.

The strategic implication for advocacy and funding is that the highest-leverage current investments are at the *Tier 1* margin: beneficial-ownership transparency, Pillar Two enforcement, agency-level antitrust, and the institutional and intellectual infrastructure for the Tier 2 substantive battles to come.

## Engaging the case against acting

The strategy is built to survive the [case against acting](/current-state/justifications-for-inequality/) rather than to ignore it. Each justification is strongest against a goal this strategy does not hold and weakest against the goal it does. The [price-of-growth](/current-state/justifications-for-inequality/#inequality-as-the-price-of-growth) and [desert](/current-state/justifications-for-inequality/#desert-and-entitlement) objections bear on taxing labor and effort; the strategy's tax weight falls on inherited and unrealized capital. The [mechanical-not-extractive](/current-state/justifications-for-inequality/#inequality-is-mechanical-not-extractive) objection defends returns to genuine scale; the antitrust and labor levers target market power and monopsony, not efficiency. The [cure-is-worse](/current-state/justifications-for-inequality/#the-cure-is-worse-than-the-disease) objection warns of capture; the prioritization explicitly weights capture-resistance and reversibility, and the democratic-reform lever attacks the capture channel head-on. The [sufficiency](/current-state/justifications-for-inequality/#the-wrong-target-sufficiency-not-the-gap) and [global-convergence](/current-state/justifications-for-inequality/#global-inequality-is-already-falling) objections are conceded where they are right — absolute poverty matters, and between-country gaps are falling — and set aside where they miss the target, which is the domestic conversion of concentrated wealth into political power. What survives is not the maximal egalitarian program but exactly this one: bounded, capture-aware, aimed at the convertible stock.

## Gender and intersecting inequality

Wealth and power concentration is not neutral across social position, so a strategy to reduce it has to be judged on those dimensions, and judged at the points where they intersect. Systematic accounts of intersectionality track disadvantage across a set of axes — class, gender, race and ethnicity, caste, nationality and migration status, disability, age, sexuality, and religion ([Collins & Bilge, *Intersectionality*, 2020](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Intersectionality,+2nd+Edition-p-9781509539673)) — and which of them carries the most economic weight varies by society: caste in South Asia, indigeneity in the Andes and Mesoamerica, race in the United States and Brazil, migration status across the Gulf and Europe. Gender cuts across all of them. Women earn under 35% of world labour income, a share that has risen only a few points since 1990 ([World Inequality Report 2022](https://wir2022.wid.world/executive-summary/)).

Looking where the axes intersect matters because the pattern Kimberlé Crenshaw named intersectional holds: the joint position is often worse off than either axis alone would predict ([Crenshaw 1989](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/uclf/vol1989/iss1/8/); [Crenshaw 1991](https://doi.org/10.2307/1229039)). It recurs across very different societies. In India, Scheduled-Caste and -Tribe women carry caste and gender disadvantage together. Their realized wages are among the lowest of any group, and India's gender wage gaps are driven mostly by labour-market discrimination rather than differences in schooling ([Agrawal 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12651-013-0152-z), on 2004–05 data); more recent work finds their labour-market aspirations the lowest of all caste-gender groups, a cumulative "double jeopardy" ([Sarkar, Chakravorty & Lyonette 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/13600818.2025.2570943)). Roughly three-quarters of the world's migrant domestic workers, the most precarious tier of the global care economy, are women ([ILO 2024](https://www.ilo.org/topics-and-sectors/domestic-workers/migrant-domestic-workers)). Women with disabilities are half as likely to hold a job as men without disabilities ([UN Women](https://www.unwomen.org/en/what-we-do/women-and-girls-with-disabilities/facts-and-figures)). And in the United States, an outsized arena for the same structure, the 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances puts the median wealth of a never-married Black woman at $3,300 and a never-married Latina at $5,600, against $40,000 for a never-married white man, roughly eight and fourteen cents on his dollar ([Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis 2024](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2024/mar/gender-wealth-gap-never-married-adults-shrank)).

The evidence is not spread evenly across these axes. Wealth and income data are richest for race and gender in a handful of high-income countries, thinner for caste and indigeneity, and close to absent for sexuality, religion, and age across most of the world. Where sexual-orientation discrimination has been studied it raises the poverty risk of sexual minorities ([World Bank & ERA 2023](https://www.worldbank.org/en/region/eca/publication/economic-cost-of-exclusion-lgbti)), which is suggestive, though the coverage spans only a few countries. These gaps are themselves a finding: where a hierarchy goes unmeasured it should be treated as an open question, because silence in the statistics is not evidence of equality.

This sharpens the design test for every lever in the strategy. The class-universal instruments — progressive and inheritance taxation, antitrust, labour power, international tax coordination — narrow the overall concentration of wealth, and because that concentration runs along these hierarchies they shrink the gaps as a by-product. What they do not reach on their own is the compounded bottom, where the households with least to protect or build on are disproportionately women at the intersection of whatever local hierarchy gender meets. The strategy therefore pairs universal levers with *targeted* ones and asks of each whether it reaches that joint position: in the United States, wealth-progressive baby bonds, fair-lending enforcement, and equal voting access; elsewhere, the analogue is whatever reaches Dalit women, Indigenous women, or migrant domestic workers a single-axis remedy would pass over. The test holds everywhere: whether the tool reaches the joint position or only the margins that single-axis versions already cover.
