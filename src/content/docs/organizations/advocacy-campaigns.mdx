Advocacy organizations are where research meets the legislative calendar. They sit downstream of the institutes profiled in [Research and Policy Institutes](/organizations/research-policy-institutes/) and upstream of legislators and regulators. They differ from think tanks in that their primary outputs are *political pressure* — coalitions, mobilization, public-facing campaigns, and direct lobbying — rather than research.

The most globally visible is **[Oxfam International](https://www.oxfam.org/)**, whose annual Davos-timed inequality reports (the "Survival of the Richest" series and successors) have done more than any other communications product to put global inequality on the elite agenda. **[Tax Justice Network](https://taxjustice.net/)** (founded 2003) straddles the research/advocacy line (covered also in the previous page). **[Fight Inequality Alliance](https://www.fightinequality.org/)** (founded 2016; 500+ organizational members across 50+ countries) is a global coalition of national civil-society groups coordinating around tax justice and wealth taxation.

In the U.S., **[Americans for Tax Fairness (ATF)](https://americansfortaxfairness.org/)** (founded 2012) is the central coalition pushing federal tax reform, comprising more than 420 national, state, and local endorsing organizations; **[Patriotic Millionaires](https://patrioticmillionaires.org/)** is a high-net-worth signatory organization whose comparative advantage is providing the elite cover that closes feasibility gaps on tax proposals; **[Demand Progress](https://demandprogress.org/)** and **[Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/)** cover the democratic-reforms agenda (campaign finance, antitrust, corporate transparency). **Communications Workers of America**, **SEIU**, and the **AFL-CIO** anchor the labor-power campaigns, with the **[Economic Security Project](https://www.economicsecurityproject.org/)** focusing on predistribution.

## The Davos communications cycle and Oxfam's annual reports

The most successful single advocacy product in inequality politics is Oxfam's annual Davos report, timed to the World Economic Forum each January. The full series 2014–2026 is documented in [Global Wealth Concentration](/current-state/global-wealth-concentration/); the operational logic of the Davos cycle is worth examining separately.

The cycle works through a coordinated package of:

1. **A pre-WEF report** (released the Sunday before WEF opens) with a memorable headline statistic ("8 men = bottom 50%"; "5 richest doubled fortunes since 2020"; "$18.3T billionaire wealth").
2. **Methodology notes** published alongside the headline that allow careful press treatment of the numbers (and are largely ignored by the popular press).
3. **Coordinated press outreach** to Reuters, AP, Bloomberg, Financial Times, and major broadsheets in the major Oxfam markets.
4. **A spokesperson lineup** that can deliver the message in multiple registers — Oxfam executive directors, expert advisors (often the underlying researchers from EU Tax Observatory or WID), and credible business-side defectors (Patriotic Millionaires, Davos signatories).
5. **Follow-on advocacy** with the year's specific policy ask (UN Tax Convention support; billionaire minimum tax; corporate accountability legislation).

The strategic logic is that **WEF generates more concentrated elite media attention than any other annual moment in the global calendar** — political leaders, finance executives, and global media are all in the same place focused on the same issues. The Davos cycle exploits this attention to inject inequality framing directly into elite discourse. The 2024 "$1.5 trillion in lost wages across 52 countries" figure (from "Inequality Inc.") and the 2025 "60% of billionaire wealth is unearned" finding (from "Takers Not Makers") were both carefully constructed for this attention cycle.

Critics argue that the Davos cycle is largely *symbolic* — it generates headlines without producing measurable policy change. The fairer reading is that it is *necessary but not sufficient*: Davos coverage shifts the framing of inequality in elite discourse, which makes substantive reforms politically more feasible over time. The 2024 G20 endorsement of the Zucman billionaire minimum tax proposal would have been substantially harder without a decade of Davos-cycle agenda-setting that had already established billionaire-tax discussion as a credible policy debate.

## Patriotic Millionaires: elite defection as a campaign vehicle

[Patriotic Millionaires](https://patrioticmillionaires.org/about/) was founded in 2010 by Erica Payne (originally as "Patriotic Millionaires for Fiscal Strength" to oppose extension of the Bush tax cuts). Payne remains Founder and President; Morris Pearl, a former managing director at BlackRock who retired in 2014 to work with the organization full-time, serves as Chair. The organization's model is unusual in U.S. advocacy: a high-net-worth membership organization (approximately 175 members as of 2024; threshold $1M+ wealth or $1M+ annual income) that publicly advocates for higher taxes on its own members.

The theory of change is **elite defection** as discussed in [Synthesis: Political Feasibility](/synthesis/political-feasibility/). The core insight is that a wealth-tax proposal opposed by 100% of the wealthy is easier for centrist politicians to oppose than the same proposal opposed by 80% of the wealthy with a vocal 20% in support. Patriotic Millionaires creates the visible 20% — providing the political cover that makes redistribution proposals viable for politicians who would otherwise face uniform elite hostility.

Notable members and signatories include Lawrence Lessig, Roberta Kaplan, Chuck Collins, Norman Lear, Whitney Tilson, Abigail Disney, and Valerie Rockefeller. The 2024 "Proud to Pay More" campaign attracted [260+ signatures from millionaires and billionaires across 17 countries](https://patrioticmillionaires.org/perspectives/proud-to-pay-more/) — a substantial expansion of the elite-defection coalition beyond the U.S.

The organization's operational outputs include:

- **Direct congressional advocacy** — testimony, briefings, member-to-member meetings.
- **Media presence** — op-eds, broadcast appearances, podcasts, the *Tax the Rich* podcast.
- **Coordinated giving** — concentrated political donations to candidates supporting the agenda.
- **International signatory campaigns** — Davos open letters; G20 advocacy.
- **Policy design** — providing high-net-worth perspective on technical tax-policy proposals.

The model has been replicated internationally (Tax Me Now in Germany; Millionaires for Humanity globally; Patriotic Millionaires UK) and provides a template for elite-defection advocacy in other countries.

## Labor coalitions: Bargaining for the Common Good

The dominant U.S. innovation in labor advocacy over the past decade is **[Bargaining for the Common Good (BCG)](https://www.bargainingforthecommongood.org/)** — a framework that emerged from a May 2014 convening at Georgetown University's Kalmanovitz Initiative for Labor & the Working Poor, drawing together SEIU, AFT, NEA, and partner community organizations. The core idea is that public-sector union contract negotiations should expand beyond traditional wage-and-benefit issues to include broader community demands (affordable housing, criminal-justice reform, climate policy, anti-displacement provisions in development agreements).

The strategic logic is that public-sector unions have unique leverage during contract negotiations and can use that leverage to advance policies that benefit both members and the broader community. The model produces:

1. **Expanded political coalitions** — community partners join labor in ways they wouldn't around narrow wage-and-benefit demands.
2. **Public legitimacy** — public-sector union demands framed in community-benefit terms attract broader support than wage demands alone.
3. **Cross-sector policy gains** — affordable housing requirements in development deals; investment of pension funds in community-benefit projects.

The model has been applied in Chicago Teachers Union (2012, 2019, 2022 strikes), Los Angeles Unified School District (UTLA 2019), and several public-employee bargaining campaigns. It is a meaningful innovation in labor strategy and a template for the broader build-power agenda in [Section 2: Labor Power](/interventions/labor-power/).

## U.S. legislative campaigns

The major U.S. inequality-related legislative vehicles in the early 2020s:

| Bill                                            | Sponsor(s)            | Status                                                  |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **For the 99.5% Act** (S.994 / H.R. 5587)       | Sanders, Gomez        | Reintroduced 2021, 2023, 2025; not advanced. Imposes 45% tax on estates >$3.5M, scaling to 65% on estates >$1B. ([sanders.senate.gov](https://www.sanders.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/Estate-Tax-Bill-2023.pdf)) |
| **Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act**                   | Warren, Jayapal       | Reintroduced 2021, 2024; 2% wealth tax on >$50M, 3% on >$1B. Not advanced. |
| **Tax Excessive CEO Pay Act**                   | Sanders, Lee          | Surcharge on corporate income tax for firms with high CEO-worker pay ratios. Not advanced. |
| **Billionaire Minimum Income Tax (Wyden)**      | Wyden                 | 25% minimum tax on income (including unrealized gains) for households >$100M. Not advanced. |
| **PRO Act**                                     | Bobby Scott           | Comprehensive labor-law reform. Passed House 2021; Senate filibuster blocked. |
| **Corporate Transparency Act (enacted)**        | Maloney/Whitehouse    | Beneficial-ownership reporting. Enacted 2021; FinCEN BOI reporting took effect Jan 2024. Substantially hollowed out: a March 2025 [FinCEN interim final rule](https://www.fincen.gov/news/news-releases/fincen-removes-beneficial-ownership-reporting-requirements-us-companies-and-us) exempted all domestic U.S. companies from BOI reporting, leaving only foreign companies subject to the requirement (see [Financial Regulation](/interventions/financial-regulation/)). |
| **Build Back Better / Inflation Reduction Act** | Various               | Inflation Reduction Act enacted 2022 with 15% corporate alternative minimum tax + IRS funding boost. |
| **Disclose Act** (multiple versions)            | Whitehouse            | Dark-money disclosure. Reintroduced repeatedly; not advanced. |

Sources: [sanders.senate.gov (For the 99.5% Act)](https://www.sanders.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/Estate-Tax-Bill-2023.pdf) · [PRO Act legislative history (Jobs With Justice)](https://www.jwj.org/legislative-history-of-the-pro-act) · [FinCEN BOI rollback (Mar 2025)](https://www.fincen.gov/news/news-releases/fincen-removes-beneficial-ownership-reporting-requirements-us-companies-and-us)

The pattern is that **most ambitious inequality-related federal legislation has not advanced** in the past decade, with the notable exceptions of the Inflation Reduction Act (which contains substantive but narrowly-targeted provisions) and the Corporate Transparency Act (transparency rather than redistribution). The Senate filibuster is the binding institutional constraint for most of these bills; Senate procedural reform is therefore a high-leverage upstream advocacy target.

## EU campaigns

The European campaign landscape differs substantially from the U.S. — campaigns target EU Council, Parliament, and Commission processes rather than national legislatures, and the policy windows are longer but more deterministic.

| Campaign                                              | Status                                              |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **EU Citizens' Initiative for a Wealth Tax** ("Tax the Rich")  | [Failed](https://euobserver.com/eu-political/arf9298ca2): collected ~300,000 signatures by the October 2024 deadline (one million required); cleared national thresholds in only 3 of 7 required member states. Did not trigger Commission action. |
| **Pillar Two implementation enforcement**             | Active; ensuring member-state transposition         |
| **Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD)** | Adopted 2024 (Directive 2024/1760); application from 2028 (delayed from 2027 in February 2025 omnibus). Requires large companies to identify and address human-rights and environmental impacts across global value chains. ([Commission CSDDD page](https://commission.europa.eu/business-economy-euro/doing-business-eu/sustainability-due-diligence-responsible-business/corporate-sustainability-due-diligence_en)) |
| **EU Adequate Minimum Wages Directive implementation** | Transposition deadline Nov 2024; 8 member states missed; ongoing advocacy for full transposition |
| **Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive (ATAD) revisions**     | Periodic updates to EU corporate tax architecture   |
| **EU Asset Recovery and Confiscation Directive**      | Adopted 2024; supports beneficial-ownership transparency enforcement |

The CSDDD is the largest single EU regulatory expansion of the past decade in scope and substantive ambition. Its application has been delayed (initial application 2028 rather than 2027) under business pressure, and the 2025 omnibus package narrowed several requirements. The advocacy challenge is to maintain implementation momentum through the 2025–2028 transposition period against ongoing rollback efforts.

The EU wealth-tax campaign — coordinated across multiple member-state-level advocacy organizations and channeled through the European Citizens' Initiative process — fell well short of the one-million-signature threshold when the "Tax the Rich" ECI closed in October 2024 with roughly 300,000 signatures. The campaign did not trigger Commission action, but it built name recognition and cross-border activist networks that advocates are repositioning toward the next G20-aligned billionaire-tax push.
