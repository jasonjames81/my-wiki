Most of this wiki argues that the concentration of wealth and power is a problem worth acting on. That argument is only worth reading if it can survive the strongest case against it. This page assembles that case: the serious, well-developed arguments — economic and philosophical — that inequality is acceptable, that it is the price of things we value, or that trying to reduce it does more harm than good.

These are not strawmen. Each is the steelman version, drawn from the economists and philosophers who made it best. After each, a **Where this lands** note gives the evidence-based response and points to where the rest of the wiki engages it in depth.

One scoping note up front. This page is about justifications for *inequality itself* and reasons not to act *at all*. The narrower question of whether specific interventions backfire — capital flight, harm to innovation, weak state capacity, populist backlash — is treated separately in [Synthesis: Risks and Tradeoffs](/synthesis/risks-and-tradeoffs/). The two pages are complementary: this one asks whether the goal is justified; that one asks whether the tools work.

## Objection matrix

| The justification | Strongest form | Where this wiki lands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price of growth | Redistribution blunts the incentives that drive effort, risk-taking, and investment | Real but bounded; the efficiency cost is modest at the rates and instruments proposed |
| Desert / entitlement | Holdings justly acquired are justly held; high pay reflects marginal product | Assumes just acquisition the data don't support; top returns are heavy with rent, inheritance, and luck |
| Wrong target | What matters morally is sufficiency and absolute poverty, not the size of the gap | Compelling for *welfare*; weak for *power*, which is positional and convertible into political control |
| Already falling | Global (between-country) inequality is declining; world poverty has collapsed | True and important — but *within-country* concentration is rising, and that is where power converts |
| Overstated | Post-tax, post-transfer, and consumption measures show far less inequality than headlines | Genuinely contested for income; the wiki's primary metric — top *wealth* shares — is robust |
| Cure is worse | Redistributive states invite rent-seeking, capture, and the conceit of "social justice" | A real risk that disciplines *how* to act, not *whether* — the synthesis weights reversibility and capture |
| Mechanical, not extractive | Superstar markets, firm scale, and assortative mating produce skew with no villain | Explains much wage dispersion; does not explain the top-0.1% capital share or its political conversion |

## Inequality as the price of growth

The oldest and most durable defense is that inequality is the by-product of incentives we cannot do without. Arthur Okun's [*Equality and Efficiency: The Big Tradeoff*](https://www.brookings.edu/books/equality-and-efficiency-the-big-tradeoff/) (1975) gave the canonical image: redistribution carries money from rich to poor in a "leaky bucket," and the leak — blunted effort, distorted investment, administrative waste — is the efficiency price of a more equal distribution. A society that insists on equality of outcome taxes away the return to skill, effort, and risk, and gets less of all three.

The microfoundations are well developed. [Lazear and Rosen](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/ucpjpolec/v_3a89_3ay_3a1981_3ai_3a5_3ap_3a841-64.htm) (1981) showed that large pay gaps can be *efficient*: tournament-style compensation, where a few big prizes sit atop a steep ladder, motivates effort across an entire workforce at low monitoring cost — so the dispersion is a feature, not a failure. [Mirrlees](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/ouprestud/v_3a38_3ay_3a1971_3ai_3a2_3ap_3a175-208.htm) (1971), founding modern optimal-tax theory, showed formally that high marginal rates shrink the very tax base they tax, so the welfare-maximizing top rate is finite and may be modest. [N. Gregory Mankiw's "Defending the One Percent"](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.27.3.21) (2013) folds these together: top incomes largely reward genuine productivity in a globalized, skill-biased economy, and clawing them back trades a real efficiency loss for a distributional gain that may not be worth it.

**Where this lands.** The tradeoff is real but its magnitude is the whole question, and the empirical magnitude is modest at the rates actually on the table. [Diamond and Saez](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.25.4.165) (2011), working the same optimal-tax machinery, estimate revenue-maximizing top rates far above current U.S. levels once realistic behavioral elasticities are used — implying substantial room to raise top rates before the efficiency leak dominates. More fundamentally, the argument conflates *income inequality* (the price of incentives) with *wealth concentration* (a stock that compounds across generations and converts into political power). Tournament theory justifies paying a CEO more than a clerk; it does not justify the untaxed inter-generational transmission of fortunes, which the incentive story barely touches. The wiki's preferred instruments — see [Progressive Taxation](/interventions/progressive-taxation/) — are chosen precisely to target the compounding stock while leaving labor-supply incentives largely intact.

## Desert and entitlement

A second defense is moral rather than economic: even if inequality had no efficiency benefit, the distribution that emerges from free exchange is *just*, because the holdings composing it were justly acquired. Robert Nozick's [*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchy,_State,_and_Utopia) (1974) made the entitlement case with the Wilt Chamberlain thought experiment: start from any distribution you consider perfectly fair, let many people each freely pay a star to watch him play, and the result is large inequality that no one can object to without objecting to the free choices that produced it. On this view, redistribution toward a preferred *pattern* requires continuously interfering with voluntary transfers — and taxing the fruits of labor to do so is, in Nozick's phrase, on a par with forced labor. Mankiw's "just deserts" framing supplies the economic analogue: in competitive markets, pay tracks marginal product, so high earners are taking roughly what they contributed.

**Where this lands.** The entitlement argument is only as strong as its premise that holdings were *justly acquired*, and that premise is where the evidence bites. A large share of top wealth derives not from voluntary exchange rewarding contribution but from inheritance, economic rent, market power, and luck — categories the wiki documents in [Power Concentration Mechanisms](/current-state/power-concentration-mechanisms/) and [Historical Trajectory](/current-state/historical-trajectory/). Marginal product is also not the same as social contribution: it is measured at the margin *in a given institutional setting*, one shaped by the incumbents' own lobbying. When a fortune originates in a captured regulatory regime or an inherited stock rather than in the Chamberlain-style voluntary transfer, the entitlement defense does not apply — and Nozick's own framework agrees, since it voids holdings traceable to injustice in acquisition or transfer.

## The wrong target: sufficiency, not the gap

A more radical move concedes the facts but denies that inequality is the thing that matters. Harry Frankfurt's ["Equality as a Moral Ideal"](https://doi.org/10.1086/292761) (1987) and his later book [*On Inequality*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691167145/on-inequality) (2015) argue for *sufficiency*: what is morally important is that everyone have enough, not that everyone have the same. To care about the gap *as such*, on this view, is to commit a kind of error — it makes the badness of a poor person's life depend on facts about a rich stranger, which gets the moral structure wrong. Derek Parfit's ["Equality and Priority"](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9329.00041) (1997) sharpens the point with prioritarianism: we should give extra weight to the worse-off, but that is a reason to *raise the floor*, not to *compress the distribution*. And Angus Deaton's [*The Great Escape*](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691153544/the-great-escape) (2013) supplies the empirical optimism: the dominant fact of modern history is the dramatic rise in absolute living standards, health, and longevity — progress that often *generates* inequality as some escape poverty first.

**Where this lands.** The sufficientarian case is strong for *welfare* and largely accepted here: absolute poverty is worse than relative position, and the wiki does not treat a smaller Gini as good in itself. But the wiki's concern is not the welfare gap — it is *power*. Concentrated wealth is positional in a way consumption is not: it converts into disproportionate political voice, legal protection, and agenda-setting control, as documented in [Power Concentration Mechanisms](/current-state/power-concentration-mechanisms/). Raising the floor does nothing to dilute that conversion; only the relative share at the top does. Sufficiency answers "is anyone destitute?" The wiki is asking "who governs?" — and for that question the gap, not the floor, is the relevant variable.

## Global inequality is already falling

Why intervene against a problem that is solving itself? Branko Milanovic's work on global inequality (see [*Global Inequality*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branko_Milanovi%C4%87), 2016) produced the famous "elephant curve": over 1988–2008 the largest real income gains went to the emerging-economy middle class, hundreds of millions of whom rose as China and India converged on the rich world. Measured *between* countries, global inequality has been falling for a generation. [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/extreme-poverty) documents the corollary: the share of humanity in extreme poverty fell from roughly four-fifths in 1820 to under a tenth today, most of the decline within living memory. Against that backdrop, fixation on the top 1% can look parochial.

**Where this lands.** This is true and genuinely important — and it is about a different distribution. Between-country convergence has indeed fallen; *within-country* concentration has risen across most large economies since 1980, the trajectory traced in [Historical Trajectory](/current-state/historical-trajectory/). The elephant curve's other feature is its raised tail: the global top 1% captured a large share of total growth even as the global middle gained. And the power-conversion mechanism the wiki cares about operates *within* national political systems, where the relevant question is the domestic top share, not the global one. Falling between-country inequality is excellent news for poverty and irrelevant to whether a domestic oligarchy is forming.

## The numbers are overstated

A measurement objection: the headline series everyone cites are pre-tax and pre-transfer, and they overstate how much inequality households actually experience. [Auten and Splinter](https://doi.org/10.1086/728741) (2024, *JPE*) rebuilt U.S. top-income shares using tax data with consistent treatment of tax-base changes and government transfers, and found top shares both lower in level and far flatter in trend since 1980 than the influential Piketty-Saez-Zucman series — on their post-tax measure, the rise in top shares nearly disappears. On the consumption side, [Meyer and Sullivan](https://cid.harris.uchicago.edu/files/2023/04/Meyer-Sullivan-2023-JPE.pdf) (2023, *JPE*) find that consumption inequality rose far less than income inequality, implying material living standards at the bottom improved more than income statistics suggest. If the best-measured quantities barely moved, the case for action weakens.

**Where this lands.** The measurement debate is real and unresolved *for income* — and the wiki concedes it, treating income figures as a contested secondary metric in [Measurement Frameworks](/current-state/measurement-frameworks/). But the Auten-Splinter critique bears on *income flows*, which taxes and transfers genuinely compress; it bears much less on *wealth stocks*, the wiki's primary metric, where even conservative estimates show pronounced and rising top-decile and top-percentile concentration. Consumption inequality being flat is consistent with wealth concentration being high: top incomes are saved, not consumed, which is exactly how they compound into the convertible stock the wiki tracks in [Global Wealth Concentration](/current-state/global-wealth-concentration/). Pointing at flat consumption to dismiss wealth concentration answers a question no one asked.

## The cure is worse than the disease

Even granting the problem, a libertarian and public-choice tradition argues that the redistributive state is the greater danger. Friedrich Hayek's [*Law, Legislation and Liberty, Vol. 2: The Mirage of Social Justice*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law,_Legislation_and_Liberty) (1976) argues that "social justice" is literally meaningless applied to the unintended outcomes of a market process — outcomes are not *chosen* by anyone and so are neither just nor unjust — and that pursuing a preferred distribution requires discretionary power that corrodes the rule of law. The public-choice school adds the mechanism: [Buchanan and Tullock's *The Calculus of Consent*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Calculus_of_Consent) (1962) shows how redistributive politics becomes a tool for majorities and organized interests to exploit others, and [Tullock's](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1465-7295.1967.tb01923.x) (1967) founding rent-seeking analysis shows that the prospect of redistribution attracts wasteful lobbying that can dissipate the transfer itself in social cost. The agency built to fight concentrated wealth, on this view, becomes the next thing concentrated wealth captures.

**Where this lands.** This is the most serious objection, because its mechanism is the same one the wiki invokes against concentrated wealth: capture. It is taken as a binding design constraint, not a refutation — it argues about *how* to act, not *whether*. The synthesis explicitly weights reversibility and capture-resistance when ranking interventions (see [Risks and Tradeoffs](/synthesis/risks-and-tradeoffs/) and [Political Feasibility](/synthesis/political-feasibility/)), favoring simple, broad, hard-to-game instruments over discretionary ones. The Hayekian point that market outcomes are "unchosen" also cuts the other way: the rules of property, inheritance, corporate law, and tax are themselves chosen and continually rewritten, often by the incumbents who benefit — so declining to act is not neutrality but a choice to keep the current rules.

## Inequality is mechanical, not extractive

A final defense removes the villain entirely: much measured inequality is the mechanical output of technology, scale, and sorting, with no exploitation to correct. Sherwin Rosen's ["The Economics of Superstars"](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/aeaaecrev/v_3a71_3ay_3a1981_3ai_3a5_3ap_3a845-58.htm) (1981) showed that when technology lets the best performer serve a global market at near-zero marginal cost, tiny differences in talent translate into enormous differences in reward — skew with no wrongdoing. [Gabaix and Landier](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/123/1/49/1889400) (2008) argue that the explosion in CEO pay tracks the growth in firm size in a competitive assignment market, not an increase in rent extraction: bigger firms make the best manager's marginal decisions worth more. And [Greenwood, Guner, Kocharkov, and Santos](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.104.5.348) (2014) show that rising *assortative mating* — high earners marrying each other — mechanically raises household income inequality with no change in the labor market at all.

**Where this lands.** These forces are real and explain a meaningful share of *wage and household* dispersion — which is one reason the wiki does not treat all inequality as illegitimate. But they explain the shape of the upper-middle and professional distribution far better than they explain the top 0.1%, where the income is predominantly *capital*, not labor, and where the relevant question is concentration of *ownership*, not dispersion of *salaries*. Superstar and scale effects also do not touch the political-conversion mechanism: a mechanically-earned billion buys the same agenda-setting power as an inherited one. That some inequality is benign and mechanical is fully consistent with the specific slice the wiki targets — concentrated, compounding, convertible wealth — being neither.

## What survives

Taken seriously, the case against acting does not collapse; parts of it are absorbed into the wiki's own position. Sufficiency is right that absolute poverty matters more than the gap. The mechanical-inequality literature is right that not all dispersion is illegitimate. The public-choice tradition is right that the cure can be captured. The measurement skeptics are right that income figures are contested. Each of these disciplines the argument — narrowing the target, raising the bar for intervention design, and ruling out the cruder egalitarian claims.

What does *not* survive is the strong conclusion that the concentration of wealth and power requires no response. Every justification above is strongest against a goal the wiki does not hold (equalizing outcomes, compressing salaries, shrinking the global Gini) and weakest against the goal it does hold: limiting the conversion of concentrated, inherited, compounding wealth into disproportionate political power. That is the question the [Interventions](/interventions/progressive-taxation/) and [Synthesis](/synthesis/core-strategy/) sections take up — now, having first conceded everything the other side gets right.
