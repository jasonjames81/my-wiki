Modern wealth concentration is best understood as the second peak of a U-shape. Concentration was extreme in the late 19th and early 20th centuries (the "Gilded Age" and the European Belle Époque), collapsed dramatically between 1914 and 1975 under the combined pressure of two world wars, the Great Depression, and high marginal taxation, then re-emerged from roughly 1980 onward. By 2020, top-1% shares in many countries had returned to levels last seen in the 1920s.

Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2014) framed this trajectory in terms of the ratio between the rate of return on capital (r) and the growth rate of the economy (g). When r > g — historically the norm outside the mid-20th-century anomaly — wealth compounds faster than incomes grow, and inherited stocks dominate the distribution. The post-war compression was driven less by organic forces and more by deliberate policy: top marginal income tax rates above 70%, capital controls under Bretton Woods, strong unions, and aggressive antitrust enforcement.

The post-1980 reversal corresponded to the unwinding of each of these policies. This is important because it suggests the reversal is *policy-driven* and therefore *policy-reversible* — a key premise for the interventions cataloged in Section 2.

## Phases of concentration

## The Piketty r > g framework and its critics

[Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2014)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_in_the_Twenty-First_Century) framed the long-run trajectory of wealth concentration in terms of the gap between the rate of return on capital (*r*) and the growth rate of the economy (*g*). When *r > g* — historically the norm outside the mid-20th-century anomaly — capital accumulates faster than incomes grow, so inherited stocks dominate the distribution and concentration rises. When *r ≤ g* (the post-1945 anomaly), capital cannot outrun economic growth and concentration falls. Piketty's empirical claim is that the post-1980 reversal of the wartime compression is consistent with a return to *r > g* conditions — and that absent deliberate countervailing policy, concentration will continue to rise toward Belle Époque levels.

The framework has been the most-debated empirical claim in inequality economics over the past decade. Three main critiques are worth taking seriously.

[Acemoglu & Robinson (2015, *JEP*, "The Rise and Decline of General Laws of Capitalism")](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.29.1.3) argue that *general* laws of capitalism (such as *r > g*) are unhelpful guides because they ignore the central role of political and economic institutions. Their empirical reanalysis runs single-equation models on 10- and 20-year averages going back to the 1800s and finds *no statistically significant evidence* that increases in (*r − g*) positively affect the top 1% income share. Their preferred frame is institutional: South Africa and Sweden experienced very similar *r* − *g* trajectories but radically different inequality outcomes, with the difference explained almost entirely by political institutions (apartheid vs. social democracy) rather than by the gap between the rate of return and growth.

[Rognlie (2015, *BPEA*, "Deciphering the Fall and Rise in the Net Capital Share")](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/2015a_rognlie.pdf) attacks the framework on a different margin. After subtracting depreciation across seven large advanced economies (U.S., Japan, Germany, France, U.K., Italy, Canada), Rognlie finds that *the only persistent rise in capital's share of income since 1948 is concentrated in housing*. Outside housing, net capital share has been roughly stable. Because housing wealth is much more broadly distributed than financial capital, the rising-capital-share story has weaker implications for top-end concentration than Piketty's framing suggests. Rognlie's policy conclusion: focus less on the capital-vs-labor split and more on the distribution of returns *within* labor income (CEO pay, finance compensation, superstar effects) and on housing supply policy.

A third critique, from Mankiw and others, is that the *r > g* mechanism is mechanically blunted by consumption (the wealthy spend), philanthropy (they give away), estate division (multiple heirs), and tax (estate and income taxes capture some of the compounding). These offsets are real and well-documented, but their magnitude is debated; Piketty's response is that they have been measurably *insufficient* to prevent the post-1980 re-concentration in any of the major advanced economies.

The fairer summary is that Piketty's *empirical observations* about wealth concentration are robust and have not been overturned, while his *causal mechanism* (*r > g* as the dominant driver) is genuinely contested. The institutional-political account that emerges from Acemoglu, Rognlie, and the post-2014 literature implies that the post-1980 reversal was substantially *policy-driven* — which is the central premise for the interventions cataloged in [Section 2](/interventions/progressive-taxation/).

## The role of war, inflation, and policy in the 20th-century compression

Whatever one's view of *r > g*, the empirical fact of the mid-20th-century compression is uncontested. Top wealth shares in advanced economies fell by roughly half between 1914 and 1975 — the largest sustained reduction in inequality in modern economic history. The mechanisms behind this collapse, however, are genuinely debated, with implications for whether anything similar could be engineered today by peaceful means.

[Walter Scheidel's *The Great Leveler* (Princeton 2017)](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691165028/the-great-leveler) advances a confronting thesis: across all of human history, only **four** mechanisms have produced large, durable reductions in inequality, and all four are catastrophic — *mass-mobilization warfare, transformative revolution, state failure, and lethal pandemics*. Scheidel's evidence runs from Han China through the Roman Empire, Mao's China, and the post-1914 Western experience, and consistently finds that peaceful, policy-driven leveling has been small in magnitude and short in duration compared to the leveling produced by violent shocks. The 20th-century compression in his account was driven primarily by the destruction of capital stocks in the world wars, the inflation of wartime bonds and savings, and the political opening that followed.

The Scheidel thesis has been pushed back on by several economic historians who argue that *peaceful* policy-driven leveling has been historically underestimated. Finland 1850–1900 saw substantial reductions in inequality without war or revolution, driven by educational expansion, cooperative institutions, and progressive taxation. Several Scandinavian and continental European cases follow similar patterns. The Scheidel reply is that these cases are real but small in magnitude relative to the war-driven compressions of 1914–1945.

The policy-vs-shock question is not just historical — it bears directly on the political-feasibility analysis in [Synthesis: Political Feasibility](/synthesis/political-feasibility/). If Scheidel is right that only catastrophes produce major leveling, the agenda in this wiki is much harder than its proponents acknowledge. If the more optimistic peaceful-leveling cases are generalizable, the agenda is feasible at the policy margin. The honest middle position is that the *largest* historical levelings have been catastrophic, but that *meaningful* peaceful levelings are well-documented and that the post-war compression was the result of *both* war shocks and the deliberate post-war policy regime (capital controls, progressive taxation, strong unions, antitrust enforcement) that made the compression durable through 1980.

The role of *inflation* deserves separate attention. The 1914–1945 compression was driven not only by capital destruction but by the wholesale erosion of bondholder wealth through wartime and post-war inflation. The U.S. price level roughly doubled from 1939 to 1948; bondholders earning fixed nominal returns lost roughly half their real wealth. The 1970s U.S. inflation had similar (though smaller) effects. Inflation is a regressive transfer for households *with* fixed-income wealth, so it tends to hit middle-class savers harder than the very wealthy whose portfolios are weighted toward equities and real assets — but for the rentier class specifically, it can be devastating.

## Country variation

The compression-then-reversal pattern is broadly shared across advanced economies but the magnitudes vary substantially. The country with the largest peak-to-trough compression and the largest subsequent reversal is the United States. The countries with the smallest reversals are the Nordics and Japan, suggesting that the institutional regime built between 1945 and 1980 is more durable in those settings.

| Country     | Top 1% wealth share, 1910 peak | Top 1% wealth share, 1975 trough | Top 1% wealth share, recent | Reversal magnitude |
| ----------- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------ |
| United States | ~45%                         | ~22%                             | ~32–35%                     | Large              |
| United Kingdom | ~70%                        | ~20%                             | ~22%                        | Modest             |
| France      | ~55%                           | ~22%                             | ~22%                        | Modest             |
| Sweden      | ~50%                           | ~20%                             | ~25%                        | Modest             |
| Germany     | ~45%                           | ~25%                             | ~28%                        | Modest             |
| Japan       | ~35%                           | ~15%                             | ~18%                        | Small              |
| Russia      | n/a                            | (Soviet era)                     | ~50% (post-1990)            | Catastrophic       |
| China       | n/a                            | (Mao era)                        | ~30% (2015)                 | Catastrophic       |

Sources: WID country files; figures are approximate and reflect different data quality across periods. The U.S. and U.K. figures pre-WWI come partly from estate-multiplier methods on inheritance tax records; the post-1975 figures use modern DINA methodologies.

The Lindert & Williamson account of the U.S. specifically ([*Unequal Gains: American Growth and Inequality since 1700*, Princeton 2016](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691170497/unequal-gains)) emphasizes that American inequality has risen in *two* great waves — 1774–1860 and 1970s–present — with the mid-20th century being a relatively brief egalitarian interlude rather than the historical norm. This framing has implications for political-feasibility expectations: if the compression was the historical exception, restoring it requires extraordinary effort.

The Russian and Chinese trajectories are genuinely *catastrophic* re-concentrations. Post-Soviet Russia's top 1% wealth share rose from negligible (under Soviet egalitarianism) to ~50% by 2010, the fastest re-concentration on record — driven primarily by privatization-era oligarch capture of formerly state-owned assets. China's top 1% share roughly doubled from 1995 to 2015 (see [Global Wealth Concentration](/current-state/global-wealth-concentration/)). Both cases illustrate that wealth concentration can rise faster than peaceful policy regimes can build to constrain it.

## The post-1980 policy reversals and their cumulative effect

The post-1980 re-concentration was driven by the simultaneous unwinding of each component of the post-war policy regime. No single reversal explains the whole pattern; the combination is what matters.

| Policy lever                     | Pre-1980 norm                            | Post-1980 reversal                                     | Cumulative effect                                  |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Top marginal income tax rate     | 70–90% (US, UK, France, Japan)           | 28–50% by 2024                                         | Large; restored top-end accumulation               |
| Estate / inheritance tax         | High effective rates, broad base         | Exemptions raised dramatically; many countries abolished | Large; preserved intergenerational transfers       |
| Capital controls (Bretton Woods) | In force; cross-border capital restricted | Dismantled 1971–1990s                                  | Large; enabled tax-base mobility                   |
| Antitrust enforcement            | Active; structural cases pursued         | Permissive (Chicago school)                            | Large; enabled market and labor monopsony power    |
| Union density / coverage         | 30%+ private sector (advanced economies) | 6–15% in most advanced economies by 2020s              | Large; weakened wage-bargaining counter-pressure   |
| Financial regulation             | Glass-Steagall + heavy oversight         | Repeals 1980s–2000; deregulation                       | Moderate; enabled financial-sector rents           |
| Corporate tax rate               | ~45% global average                      | ~23% global average by 2020s                           | Moderate; shifted tax burden to labor              |
| Public-sector employment / wage compression | Large, equalizing            | Privatization, contracting-out                         | Moderate; reduced internal wage compression        |

The cumulative magnitude of these reversals is consistent with the scale of the observed re-concentration. [IMF research (Furceri, Loungani, Ostry et al.)](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2016/wp16160.pdf) attributes roughly equal shares of the post-1980 inequality rise to (a) trade and financial globalization, (b) labor-market deregulation and union decline, (c) tax-system regression, and (d) skill-biased technological change. The first three are policy-driven and reversible; the fourth is a structural force whose distributional effect can be substantially mitigated by the first three.

The interaction between reversals matters. Lower top tax rates increased the *return* on financial-sector and CEO compensation, which increased the *political* incentive of the top to defend the deregulatory regime. Weakened antitrust enforcement created larger firms with more lobbying capacity, which used that capacity to defend the lower-tax regime. Capital mobility increased the credibility of tax-base flight threats, which reduced the political feasibility of higher rates. The reversals reinforced each other in the same way the post-war regime reinforced itself.

This is the empirical case for the integrated four-pillar strategy in [Synthesis: Core Strategy](/synthesis/core-strategy/): if the reversals reinforced each other, restoring any single pillar in isolation will be undermined by the others. A coherent restoration has to be at least partially simultaneous across multiple levers.
