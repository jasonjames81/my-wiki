Concentrated wealth is only politically interesting because it converts into concentrated power. This page enumerates the conversion mechanisms — the specific channels through which a top-1% balance sheet becomes outsized influence over laws, regulations, and public discourse. Each channel has a corresponding intervention discussed in Section 2.

Empirically, the strongest evidence for wealth-to-power conversion comes from studies of policy responsiveness (Gilens & Page 2014; Bartels 2008; Achen & Bartels 2016), which show that U.S. policy outcomes correlate strongly with the preferences of the top income decile and weakly or not at all with the preferences of the median voter once elite preferences are controlled for. Similar findings have been replicated in several European democracies (Elsässer et al. 2021; Persson & Gilljam 2017).

The mechanisms below are not exhaustive, but they capture most of the conversion pathway from wealth to durable political advantage.

## The Gilens & Page methodology and its critics

The most-cited single piece of evidence for wealth-to-power conversion is Gilens & Page's 2014 paper ["Testing Theories of American Politics: Elites, Interest Groups, and Average Citizens"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/testing-theories-of-american-politics-elites-interest-groups-and-average-citizens/62327F513959D0A304D4893B382B992B), published in *Perspectives on Politics*. The paper analyzed 1,779 U.S. policy questions on which national survey data measured both elite (top-income-decile) and median-voter preferences between 1981 and 2002, then tested which group's preferences best predicted enacted policy outcomes. The headline finding was that *elite* preferences and *organized business interest group* preferences both had statistically significant independent effects on policy adoption, while *median voter* preferences had a near-zero independent effect once elite preferences were controlled for.

The result was extraordinary on its face — a claim that one of the world's longest-running democracies operates as an oligarchy on substantive policy — and produced an immediate and substantive methodological literature. The strongest critique came from [Omar Bashir 2015 in *Research & Politics*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2053168015608896), which argued that Gilens & Page's central regression — combining a linear model, a dichotomous dependent variable (policy enacted vs. not), and highly correlated independent variables (elite and median preferences correlate at ~0.78) — produced unreliable coefficient estimates that systematically *understated* median-voter influence. Bashir's reanalysis of the same dataset showed that when elites and median voters disagreed, median voters got their preferred outcome roughly as often as elites did. A parallel critique from Enns, Branham et al. found similar results.

Gilens & Page have responded that the descriptive pattern (high concordance when groups agree, asymmetric outcomes when they disagree) survives the methodological reanalyses, and that their core claim — that the *independent* contribution of median preferences is small once elite preferences are accounted for — is robust. The fairer summary of where the literature has settled is that policy responsiveness in the U.S. is *more equal than the headline "oligarchy" framing implies, but meaningfully unequal in favor of elites and organized interests*, especially on economic policy and especially when the two groups disagree. The pattern is consistent with a wealth-to-power conversion that is real but not absolute.

[Bonica, McCarty, Poole & Rosenthal 2013 (*JEP*, "Why Hasn't Democracy Slowed Rising Inequality?")](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.27.3.103) is a complementary account that focuses on the *donor class* as the conversion mechanism. They document that the top 0.01% of donors give roughly evenly to Democrats and Republicans and that both parties have come to depend on this donor class for the majority of their funding — which constrains the policy space within which elections operate, regardless of which party wins.

## Comparative data: U.S. vs. EU vs. Nordic systems

The intuition that elite policy bias is a peculiarly American problem — driven by *Citizens United*, weak campaign finance limits, and the absence of public funding — has not survived the comparative literature of the late 2010s and 2020s. Several careful European studies have found that policy responsiveness is *more* skewed toward affluent voters in supposedly egalitarian democracies than in the U.S.

[Elsässer, Hense & Schäfer 2021 (*Journal of European Public Policy*, "Not Just Money")](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Not-just-money:-unequal-responsiveness-in-Els%C3%A4sser-Hense/fab191b0d4b2b4dec04971d80b2a0488b0fdef58) analyzed German policy outcomes 1980–2013 and found that the probability of policy change rises significantly with support from the top 10% of the income distribution, is essentially flat with respect to median support, and is indistinguishable from zero with respect to bottom-10% support. Income biases were *more* pronounced in Germany, the Netherlands, and Sweden than in the U.S. across multiple replications.

[Persson & Gilljam et al.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/psj.70083) attribute much of this comparative pattern to **status-quo bias**: in policy domains where high-income voters are satisfied with the existing arrangement and low-income voters want change, the high-income preference for "no change" is structurally easier to deliver than the low-income preference for active policy reform. Across 43 countries and 130+ policy issues, this asymmetric blocking power explains most of the income-based congruence gap, rather than active "purchase" of policy by elites.

| System                       | Top-decile policy responsiveness | Median-voter responsiveness | Bottom-decile responsiveness |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| United States (Gilens & Page 2014) | High                       | Near-zero independent effect | Near-zero                    |
| United States (Bashir 2015 reanalysis) | High                   | Moderate (when disagreement) | Low                          |
| Germany (Elsässer et al. 2021) | High                           | Near-zero                   | Near-zero                    |
| Netherlands (Elsässer et al. 2021) | High                       | Low                         | Near-zero                    |
| Sweden (Elsässer et al. 2021)  | High                           | Low                         | Near-zero                    |
| Cross-country (Persson & Gilljam) | Moderate (status-quo skew) | Moderate                    | Lower                        |

The comparative finding has two implications for the strategy in this wiki. First, fixing American campaign finance alone would not eliminate the wealth-to-power gap — analogous gaps exist in countries with much stricter financing rules. Second, *which* mechanism dominates varies by country: campaign finance and lobbying matter more in the U.S., while informal elite networks, civil-service composition, and policy-agenda gatekeeping matter more in Northwest Europe.

## Regulatory capture: finance post-2008

The 2007–2009 financial crisis produced an unusually clean case study of regulatory capture, because the crisis was severe enough to force public reckoning with how regulators had failed to constrain the financial sector and because the post-crisis legislative response (Dodd-Frank in the U.S., Basel III internationally) created enough new rules to measure subsequent capture in real time.

The pre-crisis empirical record is now well-documented. [Igan, Mishra & Tressel (2012, *IMF WP*)](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2016/12/31/A-Fistful-of-Dollars-Lobbying-and-the-Financial-Crisis-23479) constructed a panel of U.S. financial-firm lobbying expenditures and mortgage-lending behavior across 1999–2007 and found that more-intensive lobbyers (a) originated mortgages with higher loan-to-income ratios, (b) securitized more aggressively, (c) grew their mortgage portfolios faster, and (d) suffered higher delinquency rates in 2008. The pattern is consistent with lobbying *causing* loosened oversight that enabled riskier lending — not with risky lenders happening to lobby more for unrelated reasons.

[Igan & Lambert (2019, *IMF WP*, "Bank Lobbying: Regulatory Capture and Beyond")](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2019/wpiea2019171-print-pdf.pdf) extends the analysis through the post-Dodd-Frank period and finds that lobbying intensity remained predictive of laxer treatment by examiners, lower stress-test failure rates, and faster issuance of regulatory waivers. The post-crisis regulatory architecture was substantially weakened between 2017 and 2020 (Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act of 2018; Volcker Rule rollbacks; tailoring of CCAR thresholds), with the rollbacks closely tracking financial-sector lobbying priorities.

The clearest mechanism in the financial-regulation case is the *revolving door*: senior agency staff move to industry positions at multiples of public-sector compensation, creating both ex-ante incentives to soften enforcement and ex-post informational flows from agency to firm. Post-crisis cooling-off period reforms have narrowed but not closed this channel.

## Regulatory capture: antitrust 1980–2020

The antitrust collapse is even more dramatic than the financial-regulation case, both in measured magnitude and in the consensus among recent scholars about its causes. [Lancieri, Posner & Zingales (2022, NBER WP, "The Political Economy of the Decline of Antitrust Enforcement in the United States")](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30326) provides the most comprehensive recent quantification:

- **DOJ monopolization, exclusionary practice, and vertical restriction cases dropped from 221 (1955–1979) to 22 (1980–1997)** — roughly a 90% decline in the headline categories.
- **DOJ civil antitrust cases fell from ~31/year (1955–1979) to ~16/year (1980–1997)** and to fewer than 10/year by the late 2010s.
- **Total DOJ antitrust lawsuits fell from ~100/year in the early 1980s to ~25 in 2018.**

The Lancieri/Posner/Zingales causal account is striking because it explicitly *rejects* the standard "academic ideas drove the change" story (Chicago School consumer-welfare framework persuaded the bench and bar): they find no evidence that voter preferences turned against antitrust, no evidence that nominees expressed Chicago-school skepticism in confirmation hearings, and conclude that the decline was engineered by special interests using "revolving doors, lobbying, campaign donations, and training programs" to shape regulator behavior. Chicago school doctrine was, in their account, the *vehicle* big business used to rationalize a result it sought for unrelated commercial reasons.

The 2021–2024 reversal under FTC Chair Lina Khan and the EU Digital Markets Act represented the largest pushback in 40 years. Whether the reversal is durable will depend on the institutional and political durability of the New Brandeisian project — see [Antitrust and Competition](/interventions/antitrust-and-competition/) for the policy-side analysis.

## Philanthropy and tax policy

The philanthropy channel is structurally different from the others on this page because it operates *with* explicit public subsidy rather than against public scrutiny. The U.S. charitable contribution deduction is a tax expenditure of roughly **$60–65 billion per year** and a projected ~$1 trillion 2024–2034 ([JCT estimates summarized in *Inside Philanthropy*](https://www.insidephilanthropy.com/home/charitable-tax-deduction-scrutiny)). Because the deduction's value scales with the donor's marginal tax rate, it subsidizes top-bracket donors more than middle-income donors per dollar given — a $1,000 donation costs a 37%-bracket donor $630 and a 12%-bracket donor $880.

Rob Reich's [*Just Giving: Why Philanthropy Is Failing Democracy and How It Can Do Better* (Princeton 2018)](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691183497/just-giving) develops the strongest sustained critique of how this subsidy interacts with wealth concentration. Reich's core argument is that the deduction's structure converts private wealth into *public influence over which public goods get produced* — research priorities, education curricula, policy-research agendas — under conditions of weak democratic accountability and lavish tax preference. Foundation governance is "largely unaccountable, often perpetual, and lavishly tax-advantaged"; the deduction "subsidizes the gifts of rich people more than those of ordinary people"; and most charitable giving "doesn't go to the needy and doesn't reduce inequality."

The empirical pattern matches the structural argument: large foundations are increasingly active in shaping K–12 education policy (Gates, Walton, Broad), academic research priorities (Koch network, Mercatus), policy-research agendas (Heritage, AEI on the right; Open Society, Hewlett on the center-left), and even local-government decision-making in some cities. The donors retain effective control of how funds are deployed during their lifetimes and can lock in their preferences in perpetuity through foundation governance — a far weaker accountability structure than democratic politics or even shareholder governance.

Reich's policy proposal — replacing the unlimited deduction with a fixed-cap charitable tax credit (e.g., $1,000) — would substantially reduce the upward distributional skew of the subsidy without eliminating support for charitable giving. The proposal has gained traction in think-tank circles but has not advanced legislatively, in part because the policy is opposed both by the wealthy (who lose the subsidy) and by the nonprofit sector (which depends on it).

The interaction between philanthropy and the other channels on this page is amplifying. Concentrated wealth funds think tanks that produce research that legitimates lobbying positions, that funds candidates who appoint regulators, who staff agencies that the funded research programs then evaluate. Each channel alone is constrained by countervailing pressures; the combination is more durable than any single mechanism. This is the "feedback loop" intuition behind the four-pillar strategy in [Synthesis: Core Strategy](/synthesis/core-strategy/).
