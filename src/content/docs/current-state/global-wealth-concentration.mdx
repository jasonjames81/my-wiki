Wealth concentration is the starting point for every other question in this wiki. If the top decile or top percentile of households holds an outsized share of net worth, then that share underwrites their disproportionate access to political voice, legal protection, and economic opportunity for the next generation. The numbers below are drawn primarily from the World Inequality Database (WID), Credit Suisse / UBS Global Wealth Reports, and Forbes' annual billionaire census.

The headline finding across these sources is consistent: the top 1% of adults globally hold somewhere between 38% and 46% of total household wealth, depending on methodology, while the bottom 50% hold roughly 1–2%. Concentration has risen in most large economies since 1980, and the share held by billionaires specifically has grown faster than any other slice.

This page summarizes the levels and trends. Causal mechanisms (why this happened) are covered in [Historical Trajectory](/current-state/historical-trajectory/), and the translation into political power is covered in [Power Concentration Mechanisms](/current-state/power-concentration-mechanisms/).

## The billionaire layer

Billionaires (Forbes count) have grown from ~2,565 in 2023 to ~2,769 in 2024 and crossed **3,000 for the first time in 2025**, holding roughly $18.3 trillion in net worth — equivalent to ~16% of global GDP. In October 2025, Elon Musk became the first individual ever to cross $500 billion in net worth. Half of this stock was either inherited outright or built on inherited capital, per UBS' Billionaire Ambitions Report; Oxfam's 2025 decomposition (see below) puts the "unearned" share — inheritance, monopoly rents, and cronyism — at roughly 60%.

## The Oxfam annual report series

Oxfam International has published a flagship inequality report every January since 2014, timed to the World Economic Forum at Davos. The series is the most-cited communications product on global wealth concentration and has done more than any other single publication to put the topic on the elite agenda. It draws primarily on the Forbes billionaire list and Credit Suisse / UBS Global Wealth Report data, with methodology notes published alongside each headline figure.

Two methodological caveats are worth flagging up front. First, Oxfam's "bottom 50%" figures use net wealth (assets minus debts), which means heavily indebted households in rich countries — a U.S. medical student with a positive lifetime earnings trajectory but six-figure loans — count as poorer than a subsistence farmer with no debt. This is a defensible accounting choice but produces more dramatic top-vs-bottom comparisons than wealth concepts that exclude student or mortgage debt. Second, Oxfam's headline "X richest people = bottom Y billion" comparisons depend sensitively on the cutoff and the year's billionaire rankings; the underlying trend (extreme top-tail concentration) is robust to methodology, but the specific numbers move year to year.

| ---- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| 2015 | Wealth: Having It All and Wanting More                          | Top 1% projected to own more than the rest of the world combined by 2016        |
| 2016 | An Economy for the 1%                                           | 62 people held wealth equal to bottom 50%                                       |
| 2017 | An Economy for the 99%                                          | 8 men held wealth equal to bottom 50% (3.6 billion people)                      |

| 2026 | Resisting the Rule of the Rich                                  | Billionaire wealth rose >16% in 2025 to $18.3T; 12 richest > bottom 50%; billionaires 4,000× more likely to hold political office |

The trajectory across the series tells a consistent story: extreme top-tail concentration accelerated during the COVID pandemic (2020–2022), kept accelerating post-pandemic (2023–2025), and is now growing faster than at any point in the series' history. The 2024 report's projection that the world would see its first trillionaire within a decade — which seemed aggressive on release — looked considerably more plausible by the 2026 report, with Musk's net worth above $500B.

The 2025 "Takers Not Makers" report introduced a useful analytical move: decomposing billionaire wealth into earned and unearned components. The 60% unearned share (inheritance + monopoly + cronyism) directly informs the prioritization in [Synthesis](/synthesis/prioritized-interventions/) — interventions that target the unearned share (inheritance taxation, antitrust, beneficial-ownership transparency) face weaker innovation-cost objections than those that target the earned share.

## Why the four data sources disagree

Headline wealth-concentration numbers vary by 5–15 percentage points across the major sources. The differences are not noise — they reflect deliberate methodological choices about *which* wealth to count, *how* to estimate it, and *where* to draw the population frame.

**World Inequality Database (WID)** uses "distributional national accounts" (DINA), combining income tax records, household surveys, and national accounts data. It applies the Saez–Zucman *capitalization method*: observed capital incomes (interest, dividends, rents) are capitalized at asset-class-specific rates of return to back out underlying wealth stocks ([Saez & Zucman 2016, *QJE*](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/saez-zucmanNBER14wealth.pdf)). WID then adds an offshore-wealth adjustment using the Zucman approach ([Zucman 2013, *QJE*](https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/Zucman2013QJE.pdf)). The result is the longest consistent time series available — back to 1913 for the U.S., 1800 for France — and the methodology that produces the highest top-share estimates for high-income countries.

**Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts (DFA)** is the U.S. official distributional series, published quarterly. It integrates the Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF, triennial, ~6,000 households) with the aggregate Financial Accounts (Z.1) so that distributional shares always sum to the official aggregate balance sheet ([Fed DFA documentation](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/efa/efa-distributional-financial-accounts.htm)). The DFA reports the U.S. top 1% net worth share at **31.7% in Q3 2025** ([FRED series WFRBST01134](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBST01134)) — meaningfully lower than WID's ~35%, primarily because the SCF cannot fully observe top-tail wealth and the DFA does not impute offshore.

**Credit Suisse / UBS Global Wealth Report** is a private-sector annual report covering ~5.4 billion adults across 200+ countries. It uses a mixed approach: SCF-style household balance-sheet data where available, plus imputation from per-capita income, financial-market depth, and other indicators where it is not. UBS reports global top-1% adult wealth shares around 45–48% in recent years ([UBS Global Wealth Report 2024](https://mediahandler.broadridgeadvisor.com/media/653843/GW_Report-Digital_09072024v2_Final_SSK_optimized.pdf)). It uses *gross household wealth* nets out *only* explicit debts, which is why bottom-50% global shares are so small (heavily indebted rich-country households drag the bottom down).

**Forbes** is not a distributional source at all — it is a journalist-curated list of the ~3,000 wealthiest individuals globally, built from public filings, valuations of private holdings, and reporting. It misses wealth held under nominees, in trusts, or by individuals who avoid disclosure (notably in China, the Gulf, and Russia). It is the *only* source that captures the very top of the distribution at individual resolution.

| Source                     | Coverage                  | Method                                | Headline U.S. top-1%   | Headline global top-1% |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------- |
| WID (DINA)                 | 130+ countries, 1913–     | Capitalization + offshore adjustment  | ~35%                   | ~38%                   |
| Fed DFA                    | U.S. only, 1989–          | SCF integrated with Z.1 aggregates    | 31.7% (Q3 2025)        | n/a                    |
| Credit Suisse / UBS GWR    | 200+ countries, 2000–     | Mixed: SCF where available + imputation | ~30%                | ~45%                   |
| Forbes Billionaires        | ~3,000 individuals        | Journalistic compilation              | n/a                    | n/a (top-tail only)    |
| Oxfam                      | Re-uses GWR + Forbes      | Re-presentation with comparisons      | n/a                    | n/a (presentation)     |

The practical implication: pick the source that matches the question. For long-run trends and offshore-inclusive estimates, use WID. For U.S. quarterly tracking, use the Fed DFA. For global cross-country headline comparisons, use the GWR. For individual identification at the top, use Forbes. Mixing them in a single comparison without methodology notes is a common source of error.

## Country-level concentration

Country-level top-1% wealth shares vary widely — but cluster into recognizable regimes. The high-income democracies span roughly 25–40%, with the U.S. at the high end of that range. The middle-income BRICS countries divide sharply: Brazil and India are at the top of the global league table on wealth concentration, while China has converged from a low-inequality starting point in 1978 to levels approaching the U.S. The Nordic countries, often cited as low-inequality models, have *higher* wealth concentration than is widely understood — they compress income distributions through taxation and transfers, but pre-tax wealth concentration remains substantial.

| Country     | Top 1% wealth share | Top 10% wealth share | Source / year                                    |
| ----------- | ------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| United States | ~31.7% (Fed DFA, Q3 2025); ~35% (WID) | ~71% (Fed DFA) | [Fed DFA](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBST01134) |
| United Kingdom | n/a top 1% (suppressed); top 10% ~43% | 43% (2020–22) | [ONS Wealth and Assets Survey, Jan 2025](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/personalandhouseholdfinances/incomeandwealth/bulletins/totalwealthingreatbritain/april2020tomarch2022/pdf) |
| France      | ~22% (2014, rising)         | ~55%                 | [Garbinti, Goupille-Lebret & Piketty 2021](https://www.insee.fr/en/statistiques/fichier/4253139/510_511_512_Garbinti_Goupille-Lebret_EN.pdf) |
| Brazil      | ~49% (2021)         | ~80%                 | [WID Brazil](https://wid.world/country/brazil/); De Rosa, Flores & Morgan 2024 |
| India       | **40.1% (2022–23)** | ~65%                 | [Bharti, Chancel, Piketty & Somanchi 2024 ("Billionaire Raj")](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-04563836) |
| China       | ~30% (2015, rising) | ~67%                 | [Piketty, Yang & Zucman 2019, *AER*](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20170973) |

Three patterns are worth noting. First, the **U.S.** is no longer a clear outlier among advanced economies — France, Germany, and the U.K. have all converged toward similar levels of wealth concentration, even as their *income* distributions remain more compressed. Second, **India** is now among the most wealth-concentrated countries on Earth: the top 1% share of 40.1% in 2022–23 is the highest in Indian recorded history and exceeds the levels of the colonial period — a finding the WID authors label "the Billionaire Raj." Third, **China**'s top 1% share roughly doubled from ~15% in 1995 to ~30% by 2015, the fastest re-concentration on record; the public-property share of national wealth fell from 70% to 30% over the same period, shifting the underlying ownership structure.

The U.K. ONS produces unusually conservative figures because the Wealth and Assets Survey under-samples the very top — academic reanalysis using tax data ([Advani et al. 2025, *European Economic Review*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0014292125001266)) suggests the ONS understates the top 1% share by 5–10 percentage points.

## Income concentration vs. wealth concentration

Income and wealth concentration are related but distinct, and conflating them produces systematic errors in policy debate. **Income** is a flow (typically annual earnings, dividends, interest, rental receipts); **wealth** is a stock (assets minus liabilities at a point in time). Wealth is consistently more concentrated than income, by a factor of roughly 2–3 in most advanced economies, because wealth compounds across the lifecycle and across generations while income does not ([Saez & Zucman 2020, *JEP*, "The Rise of Income and Wealth Inequality in America"](https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/SaezZucman2020JEP.pdf)).

| Concentration measure       | U.S. (recent) | Ratio wealth/income |
| --------------------------- | ------------- | ------------------- |
| Top 1% income share         | ~19–20%       |                     |
| Top 1% wealth share         | ~32–35%       | ~1.7×               |
| Top 0.1% income share       | ~9%           |                     |
| Top 0.1% wealth share       | ~18–20%       | ~2.0×               |
| Top 0.01% income share      | ~4%           |                     |
| Top 0.01% wealth share      | ~10%          | ~2.5×               |

The implications for intervention design are direct. Income-based policies (income tax rates, EITC, payroll-tax design) move the income distribution but only slowly affect the wealth distribution because wealth at the top is largely capital, not labor income. Wealth-based policies (estate taxes, wealth taxes, capital-gains-at-realization reform) move the wealth distribution directly. The two are complementary, not substitutes — see [Progressive Taxation](/interventions/progressive-taxation/) for the policy mapping.

A second implication: **wealth concentration responds slowly to policy changes**, on the order of decades, because wealth stocks turn over slowly. The U.S. estate tax was effectively gutted between 2001 and 2017 (exemption raised from $675K to $11.2M); the resulting wealth-concentration effects will continue compounding for decades. Conversely, a future re-strengthening would also take decades to unwind. This long-horizon dynamic makes wealth concentration a particularly path-dependent and politically durable problem.

## Offshore wealth: what gets missed

Headline concentration figures from any source likely *understate* the top tail because a meaningful share of top-decile wealth sits outside the reporting perimeter of any individual country. Zucman's foundational estimate ([Zucman 2013, *QJE* "The Missing Wealth of Nations"](https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/Zucman2013QJE.pdf)) put offshore household financial wealth at ~8% of global financial wealth, or ~$7.6 trillion in 2014; updated estimates push the figure to ~$10–12 trillion by the mid-2020s.

The distributional implications are severe because offshore wealth is *extremely* concentrated. Using leaked data from HSBC Switzerland ("Swiss Leaks") and Mossack Fonseca ("Panama Papers") matched against Scandinavian administrative records, [Alstadsæter, Johannesen & Zucman (2019, *AER*)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.20172043) document that:

- **The top 0.01% of households own ~50% of all offshore wealth.**
- **The top 0.1% own ~80%.**
- **Tax evasion rises sharply with wealth**: ~3% of personal taxes are evaded on average, but ~25–30% are evaded by the top 0.01% (households with >$45M in net wealth in Scandinavia).

Because offshore wealth is so concentrated at the top, including it raises top-share estimates substantially even in countries that are not themselves heavy users of tax havens. Norway and Sweden — high-trust, high-compliance jurisdictions — saw their top 0.01% wealth shares revised upward by roughly 25% once offshore wealth was included.

The interventions that would close this measurement gap are policy interventions: beneficial-ownership transparency, the OECD Common Reporting Standard, automatic information exchange. These are covered in [Financial Regulation](/interventions/financial-regulation/) and [Global Tax Coordination](/interventions/global-tax-coordination/). The empirical evidence ([Johannesen, Langetieg, Reck, Risch & Slemrod 2020](https://www.taxobservatory.eu/repository/tax-evasion-and-inequality/)) is that these instruments work — early-adopting jurisdictions saw 15–25% drops in offshore deposits — but coverage gaps remain (real estate, art, beneficial ownership of operating companies).

## Oxfam methodology: what is robust and what is presentation

Returning to the Oxfam annual reports surveyed earlier, it is worth being explicit about which of their figures rest on solid measurement foundations and which are presentation choices that exaggerate (or de-emphasize) particular framings. The distinction matters because Oxfam's headline numbers travel widely without their methodological context.

**Robust** (well-supported by the underlying GWR and Forbes data):
- *Total billionaire wealth* and its year-over-year growth rate. These are direct sums from Forbes, with conservative valuation methodology.
- *Number of billionaires* and the rate of new billionaire creation. Direct count from Forbes.
- *Share of new wealth captured by the top 1%* (e.g., the 2023 report's "63% of new wealth" claim). Computed from GWR distributional changes; methodology is published with the report.
- *Real-wage decline figures* (e.g., the 2024 "$1.5 trillion in lost wages across 52 countries" claim). Sourced from ILO data.

**Sensitive to presentation choices**:
- *"X richest people = bottom Y billion"* comparisons (e.g., "8 men = bottom 50%"). The X varies year-to-year with Forbes valuations; the Y depends on the bottom-50% wealth concept (which is small or even negative under the GWR's debt-inclusive definition). A 5% change in either side of the comparison produces a dramatically different headline number.
- *Bottom-50% wealth shares*. Under the GWR's net-wealth concept, U.S. medical students with student debt count as poorer than subsistence farmers, which makes bottom-50% comparisons more dramatic but less informative than they appear.
- *"On track for first trillionaire by year X"* claims. Linear extrapolations from a single year of fast top-tail growth; sensitive to which year is the base.

**Worth treating with care**:
- *Decompositions of billionaire wealth into earned vs. unearned* (the 2025 "60% unearned" figure). The categories — inheritance, monopoly power, cronyism — are defensible but require judgment calls; the *pattern* (most billionaire wealth is not from competitive entrepreneurial returns) is well-supported, but the precise 36/18/6 split is not a precise measurement.

The pattern across all of these is that Oxfam's *directional* claims — extreme top-tail concentration is rising rapidly, the post-2020 period accelerated it, much of it is unearned — are robust and well-sourced. The specific comparison numbers that travel best in headlines are more fragile. For policy work, cite the directional findings from the underlying GWR and WID data; for communications work, the Oxfam comparisons are designed to land emotionally and do so effectively.
