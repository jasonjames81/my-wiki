If [Section 1](/my-wiki/current-state/power-concentration-mechanisms/) is right that concentrated wealth produces concentrated political influence through identifiable channels — campaign contributions, lobbying, regulatory capture, media ownership — then democratic reforms are the interventions that target those channels directly. They do not redistribute wealth itself, but they reduce the political *return* on holding it, which both blunts the policy feedback loop (rich → policy → richer) and changes the political feasibility of every other intervention in this wiki.

Empirical evidence on democratic reforms is mixed, partly because reforms tend to be incremental and partly because the wealthy adapt. Campaign-finance limits, for example, are often circumvented by independent expenditures and dark-money 501(c)(4)s (post-*Citizens United* U.S.; similar patterns in other countries). However, public financing of campaigns has shown more robust effects: Seattle's Democracy Vouchers, Arizona and Maine's clean-elections programs, and several European models have all produced more economically and demographically diverse candidate pools without obvious blowback.

The strongest reforms target structural rather than transactional corruption: cooling-off periods between government service and private-sector lobbying, agency-level capacity investment (so regulators do not depend on industry for technical expertise), and transparency mandates that allow citizens and journalists to track the money. These are slower-acting but harder to reverse.

## The *Citizens United* regime and dark-money growth

The U.S. campaign-finance landscape changed substantially with *Citizens United v. FEC* (2010), which struck down restrictions on independent corporate and union expenditures in elections, and *SpeechNow.org v. FEC* (2010, D.C. Circuit), which established Super PACs (independent-expenditure-only committees with unlimited contribution limits). The combination created a parallel campaign-finance architecture outside the contribution limits and disclosure rules that govern direct contributions to candidates and parties.

The headline outcome has been an order-of-magnitude increase in outside spending, and a particularly sharp rise in **dark money** — political spending by 501(c)(4) "social welfare" organizations and similar vehicles that are not required to disclose donors. [OpenSecrets data summarized by the Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/citizens-united-explained) tracks the trajectory:

| Cycle      | Outside spending | Dark money    |
| ---------- | ---------------- | ------------- |
| 2008       | $574M            | under $5M (2006 baseline) |
| 2012       | $1.3B            | ~$300M        |
| 2020       | $3.3B            | ~$1.0B        |
| 2024       | ~$4.5B           | **$1.9B**     |

More than 60% of all politically active 501(c)(4) "social welfare" organizations have been created since 2010, after Citizens United legalized their independent campaign expenditures. In the 2024 cycle, dark money topped $1 billion in the presidential race alone, with at least $182M funneled through groups closely aligned with the two major parties' congressional leadership campaigns.

The structural problem with dark money is not the dollar magnitude alone — it is the **combination of large dollar magnitude, complete donor anonymity, and structural insulation from candidates**. A donor giving $10M to a candidate's Super PAC is theoretically subject to disclosure; a donor giving $10M to a 501(c)(4) that then transfers funds to the Super PAC is not. The result is that even the largest contributions to U.S. elections can flow without the contributor's identity ever being publicly disclosed.

The legal path forward in the U.S. is constrained: *Citizens United* would require Supreme Court reversal or constitutional amendment to overturn directly. The narrower path is to require disclosure of the *original* donors to 501(c)(4)s engaged in political activity (the "DISCLOSE Act" pathway) and to enforce existing rules against coordination between candidates and outside spending vehicles more strictly. Neither has substantively advanced legislatively as of 2026.

## Public financing models that work

The most empirically successful U.S. democratic-reform interventions in the post-*Citizens United* era have been **public financing** programs at the state and municipal level, which work *with* the constitutional framework (subsidizing constitutionally-protected speech rather than restricting it) rather than against it.

The most innovative is **Seattle's Democracy Voucher program**, established by Initiative 122 in 2015 and operating since 2017. Every Seattle voter receives four $25 vouchers per cycle that can be assigned to any qualifying candidate; the city redeems vouchers from public funds. The empirical record is mixed but more positive than skeptics expected:

- **Number of small donors expanded dramatically.** Small donors (under $100) in at-large city council races increased ~6× between 2015 and 2021. Mayoral small donors increased ~5× between 2017 and 2021. ([UW research summarized 2022](https://www.washington.edu/news/2022/05/26/seattle-democracy-vouchers-increase-donations-number-of-candidates-in-city-elections/))
- **Donor diversity by race improved meaningfully.** People of color donating to Seattle city campaigns rose from under 1% in 2013 to ~8% by 2021. The lowest-income residents became slightly *over*-represented among voucher users.
- **Candidate diversity increased.** Winning candidates were almost 8 years younger on average post-vouchers than pre-vouchers; racial and gender diversity also improved.
- **Limitations.** [Heersink, Peterson & Skovron 2024 (*APSR*)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/campaign-finance-vouchers-do-not-expand-the-diversity-of-donors-evidence-from-seattle/BD8E21A4B646DE4EA56BF8787DF0FF81) argue that voucher *users* are not as demographically representative as raw counts suggest — knowledge of and participation in the program skew higher among white, wealthier, and older Seattle residents, partially offsetting the donor-diversity gains.

The older models — **Arizona Clean Elections** (1998), **Maine Clean Election Act** (1996), **Connecticut Citizens' Election Program** (2008), New York City's matching-funds program — work on a different principle (matching small contributions at multiples, with eligibility thresholds based on small-donor counts). All have produced more diverse candidate pools and reduced average dependence on large donors. None has fully eliminated the parallel role of outside spending.

The honest summary is that public financing **changes the candidate-recruitment funnel and reduces dependence on large donors at the candidate level** but does not address the parallel outside-spending architecture. That is why public financing is necessary but not sufficient as a democratic-reform agenda.

## Lobbying disclosure: U.S. LDA, EU transparency register, U.K. regime

| Regime                                     | Year       | Mandatory? | Sanctions for breach |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------- | ---------- | -------------------- |
| **U.S. Lobbying Disclosure Act (LDA)**     | 1995 (amended 2007 by HLOGA) | Yes (over thresholds) | Civil/criminal penalties; rarely enforced |
| **EU Transparency Register**               | 2011 (revised 2021) | Mandatory for Parliament/Commission/Council activities | Removal from register; meeting refusals |
| **U.K. Register of Consultant Lobbyists**  | 2014        | Narrow scope (consultant lobbyists only) | Civil penalties up to £7,500          |
| **Canada Lobbying Act**                    | 1988 (amended 2008) | Yes (over thresholds) | Up to 2 years imprisonment |
| **Ireland Regulation of Lobbying Act**     | 2015        | Yes        | Civil penalties; criminal for repeat |

The **U.S. LDA** captures direct lobbying of legislative and executive branch officials by registered lobbyists above relatively low activity thresholds. The 2007 Honest Leadership and Open Government Act tightened reporting requirements and added quarterly disclosure. Compliance is broadly observed but enforcement is weak — most LDA violations are settled with civil penalties at small scale, and criminal prosecution is rare. The LDA's largest gap is that it does not capture *strategic advice* or *policy work* that does not include direct contact with covered officials, which has produced a substantial "shadow lobbying" sector that operates outside the disclosure regime.

The **EU Transparency Register**, jointly maintained by the Parliament, Commission, and Council since 2021, lists ~12,500 entities as of 2024 with more than half being in-house lobbyists and trade associations. The register is now mandatory for activities targeting Parliament and the Commission ([Frontiers 2024 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2024.1508017/full)), but Council-targeting lobbying remains weakly covered. Sanctions are limited to register removal and meeting refusals, with no criminal or substantial civil penalties.

The U.K. regime is unusually narrow — covering only *consultant* lobbyists (paid advocates) and not in-house corporate or NGO lobbyists — which leaves the bulk of U.K. lobbying activity entirely outside the register. The post-Brexit period has seen multiple proposed expansions, none yet enacted.

The pattern across regimes is that **disclosure systems work for the activities they cover but consistently fail to keep up with shadow lobbying, strategic advisory, and indirect influence channels**. The reform frontier is expanding scope (covering more activities) and tightening enforcement (raising sanctions and resourcing the enforcers) more than redesigning the underlying architecture.

## Revolving door: cooling-off periods

The "revolving door" is the movement of personnel between regulatory agencies and the firms they regulate. The two-way flow — agency → industry, and industry → agency — produces both ex ante incentives to soften enforcement (in expectation of post-government employment) and ex post information flows that benefit former employers.

Cooling-off periods are the standard regulatory response. Coverage and duration vary widely:

| Jurisdiction                | Period                                | Scope                                       |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **U.S. federal (Executive)** | 1–2 years (post-employment)          | Senior officials; specific matter restrictions  |
| **U.S. federal (Congress)** | 1 year (House); 2 years (Senate)      | Lobbying former colleagues                  |
| **EU Commissioners**        | 2 years                               | Lobbying on former portfolio                |
| **EU Members of Parliament**| 6 months                              | Lobbying the Parliament                     |
| **U.K. Ministers**          | 2 years                               | Reviewed by Advisory Committee on Business Appointments (ACoBA) |
| **France**                  | 3 years                               | Reviewed by Haute Autorité pour la Transparence de la Vie Publique |
| **Germany**                 | 12–18 months                          | Federal ministers; reviewed by ad hoc committee |

The 2024 EU reforms ([European Parliament 2024 briefing](https://epthinktank.eu/2024/04/29/rules-on-revolving-doors-in-the-eu-post-mandate-restrictions-on-members-of-eu-institutions-and-parliamentarians-in-member-states/)) created a new EU Body for Ethical Standards as an inter-institutional ethics enforcement mechanism, which represents the most substantial EU-level revolving-door reform in a decade. The U.S. 2007 HLOGA strengthened cooling-off periods at the federal level but with substantial gaps for "shadow" advisory work that doesn't require lobbyist registration.

The empirical evidence on cooling-off-period effectiveness is mixed. Longer periods do reduce direct lobbying contacts, but they also produce evasion: former officials work in "consulting" or "strategic advisory" capacities that fall outside lobbyist registration but still leverage agency relationships. The strongest reforms combine cooling-off periods with **continuous disclosure of post-employment activities** and **agency-side rules** restricting contact with former employees.

## Anti-corruption institutions

The fifth element of the democratic-reform agenda is the institutional infrastructure for *enforcement* — ombudsmen, inspectors general, supreme audit institutions, parliamentary oversight committees, and independent anti-corruption commissions. These are the bodies that convert formal rules into actual enforcement when they are properly resourced and independent.

| Institution type                           | Typical role                                      | Examples                          |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| **Inspector General offices**              | Internal agency oversight                         | U.S. IG community (~70 IGs)       |
| **Supreme audit institution**              | Independent audit of government accounts and programs | GAO (U.S.); ECA (EU); NAO (U.K.) |
| **Independent anti-corruption commission** | Investigative + prosecutorial                     | Hong Kong ICAC; NSW ICAC (Australia) |
| **Ombudsman**                              | Citizen complaints; administrative oversight      | Most European countries; many U.S. states |
| **Parliamentary oversight committee**      | Legislative oversight of executive                | UK Public Accounts Committee; many parliaments |
| **Supreme financial regulator**            | Financial-sector oversight                        | SEC (U.S.); ESMA (EU); FCA (U.K.) |

The cross-country pattern is that countries with strong, independent, well-resourced anti-corruption institutions (Hong Kong ICAC pre-2020; New Zealand SFO; Norway Økokrim) sustain meaningfully lower corruption levels than countries with formal but weak institutions. The institutional design that consistently works includes **secure tenure for institution heads** (multi-year terms not coterminous with the executive), **statutory budget protections** (preventing executive starvation of the institution), and **direct prosecutorial authority** (rather than referral to executive-controlled prosecutors).

The U.S. inspector-general community is a partial example. The 1978 Inspector General Act created semi-independent IG offices in most federal agencies; their independence has been periodically tested by executive removals (most prominently in 2020). The 2022 IG Reform Act strengthened protections but left the basic appointment-and-removal framework with the executive, which limits durability.

For the wealth-and-power agenda specifically, the most consequential anti-corruption institutions are the **financial regulators** (SEC, FinCEN, the regional Fed banks) and the **revenue services** (IRS Large Business and International Division; HMRC's High Net Worth Unit; equivalent units across OECD jurisdictions). These institutions sit at the operational frontier of progressive taxation and financial regulation; their resourcing and independence are arguably the highest-leverage democratic-reform investments because they directly determine whether the substantive policies in [Section 2](/my-wiki/interventions/progressive-taxation/) operate as designed or as nominal-only constraints.
