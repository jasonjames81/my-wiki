Domestic tax policy is constrained from above by international tax architecture. A country that raises capital taxes unilaterally faces capital flight to lower-tax jurisdictions; a country that taxes multinational profits unilaterally faces profit-shifting and reorganization. This is not a hypothetical: it is the dominant explanation for the post-1980 fall in statutory corporate tax rates (from a global average of ~45% to ~23%) and the persistence of low effective tax rates on the very top of the wealth distribution.

The OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework's Two-Pillar agreement (2021–2025) is the most ambitious coordination attempt to date. **Pillar Two** establishes a global minimum corporate tax of 15%, with a top-up mechanism that lets any signatory country collect tax that would otherwise be undertaxed elsewhere. As of 2026 it is in force in the EU, U.K., Japan, Korea, and a growing list of others; U.S. implementation status remains the major open question. **Pillar One** (reallocation of taxing rights for digital giants) has progressed more slowly and may be supplanted by unilateral digital services taxes.

Beyond corporate tax, the 2024 G20 endorsement of Zucman's **billionaire minimum tax** proposal — a 2% annual floor on the wealth of billionaires, coordinated to prevent migration arbitrage — marks the first time wealth-tax coordination has been seriously on the international agenda. A parallel **UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation** is in negotiation, championed by the African Group and resisted by several OECD members; it could shift rule-setting authority away from the OECD toward a more representative forum.

## OECD Pillar One and Pillar Two mechanics

The OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework's [Two-Pillar Agreement (October 2021)](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/global-minimum-tax.html) was the largest restructuring of international tax rules since the 1920s. It addresses two distinct problems through two distinct instruments.

**Pillar One** addresses the *allocation* of taxing rights — specifically, the long-standing question of where the largest digital and consumer-facing multinationals should be taxed. Under existing OECD rules dating to the 1920s, a multinational is generally taxed where it has *physical presence* (a "permanent establishment"). Digital business models break this framework: a U.S.-headquartered platform serves customers in France through servers in Ireland, with no physical French presence and therefore no French taxing rights even on French-sourced revenue. Pillar One reallocates a fraction of the largest multinationals' profits to *market jurisdictions* (countries where customers are) regardless of physical presence.

Pillar One has stalled. Implementation requires a multilateral convention (originally targeted for ratification by ~140 jurisdictions); negotiations have repeatedly missed deadlines (originally 2023, then 2024, now indefinite). Several countries have proceeded with **unilateral digital services taxes (DSTs)** — France, Italy, Spain, U.K., India, several others — typically 2–5% of digital revenue from in-country users. The U.S. has consistently opposed DSTs (including under both Trump and Biden administrations) and threatened tariff retaliation; the Pillar One framework was supposed to replace DSTs with the multilateral allocation mechanism, but its stalling has left the unilateral patchwork in place.

**Pillar Two** addresses the *level* of taxation — specifically, the bottom-line floor on effective corporate tax rates. Under Pillar Two, multinationals with annual revenue above €750 million face a **15% global minimum effective tax rate** on their profits in each jurisdiction. If a country chooses to tax at less than 15%, other signatory countries can collect the *top-up* tax that would otherwise be foregone. This creates a powerful incentive against further race-to-the-bottom corporate tax competition: a country that lowers its rate below 15% loses the revenue to other signatories rather than gaining tax-base inflow.

Implementation status as of early 2026 ([OECD Pillar Two implementation tracker](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/global-minimum-tax/global-anti-base-erosion-model-rules-pillar-two.html)):

- **EU 27 member states**: enacted via 2022 Council Directive; Income Inclusion Rule (IIR) and Qualified Domestic Minimum Top-up Tax (QDMTT) effective 1 January 2024.
- **U.K., Norway, Switzerland, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, Canada, Vietnam, Malaysia, Singapore, Hong Kong, others**: enacted, in various stages of effective implementation.
- **U.S.**: **In January 2026, the Treasury announced that U.S.-headquartered multinationals would be exempt from Pillar Two's requirements**, citing the existing GILTI regime as a sufficient analogue. This is the major outstanding implementation issue and creates a substantial coordination gap.
- **Undertaxed Profits Rule (UTPR)**: the most aggressive Pillar Two element (collecting top-up tax even from non-signatories) is generally delayed to 2026 or later.
- **First filings**: GloBE Information Return filings for calendar-year 2024 taxpayers are due 30 June 2026.

The Inclusive Framework agreed a "Side-by-Side" package in [January 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/12/international-community-agrees-way-forward-on-global-minimum-tax-package.html) to clarify the coordinated operation of Pillar Two given the U.S. carve-out. This is the largest live political-economy question in international tax.

## The UN Framework Convention process

The OECD/G20 process has produced substantial outputs (Pillar Two, BEPS Actions 1–15, CRS) but has been criticized — particularly by Global South countries — for being structurally weighted toward high-income country interests. The [African Group at the UN](https://financing.desa.un.org/unfcitc) has championed an alternative venue: a binding **UN Framework Convention on International Tax Cooperation (UNFCITC)**, negotiated under UN auspices where developing countries have proportionally more voice.

The institutional trajectory:

- **November 2023**: UN General Assembly Resolution 78/230 (introduced by Nigeria on behalf of the African Group) established an Ad Hoc Committee to develop terms of reference.
- **August 2024**: UNGA adopted the [Draft Terms of Reference](https://financing.desa.un.org/unfcitc) by overwhelming majority. Most OECD member states voted against or abstained; almost all African, Latin American, and Asian developing countries voted in favor.
- **2025–2027**: Member-state-led negotiation through an Intergovernmental Negotiating Committee (INC), meeting three times per year.
- **November 2025**: Third INC session in Nairobi.
- **February 2026**: Fourth INC session concluded. Two early protocols agreed: cross-border services taxation and dispute prevention/resolution.

The institutional stakes are substantial. The UN convention would be the first internationally binding tax instrument outside the OECD framework and could substantially shift rule-writing authority toward a more representative forum. Critics (mostly OECD member states) argue that the UN process risks duplicating work already done at the OECD and producing weaker, less-implementable instruments. Proponents (mostly Global South) argue that the OECD process structurally favors capital-exporting countries and that meaningful coordination requires Global South participation in rule-making, not just in rule-taking.

The political dynamics resemble the Doha Round vs. WTO debates of the 2000s: a subset of countries that finds the existing institution unresponsive opens a parallel process; the existing institution either accommodates the new demands or finds itself displaced. The next 2–3 years will determine which trajectory wealth-and-tax governance follows.

## The Zucman billionaire minimum tax

The most concrete recent proposal for international wealth-tax coordination is the **billionaire minimum tax**, developed by Gabriel Zucman for Brazil's 2024 G20 presidency and endorsed in principle by G20 finance ministers in July 2024. The [Zucman G20 Report](https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/report-g20.pdf) proposes:

- A **2% annual minimum effective tax rate** on the wealth of the world's ~3,000 billionaires.
- Coordinated implementation across signatory countries, with top-up tax collection mechanisms analogous to Pillar Two.
- Anti-migration provisions (exit taxation, residence-based liability for a transition period).
- Use of existing transparency infrastructure (CRS, FATCA, beneficial-ownership registries) for enforcement.

The revenue arithmetic ([Zucman 2024 G20 report](https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/report-g20.pdf)):

- A 2% minimum on the world's ~3,000 billionaires would raise **$200–250 billion per year**.
- Extending the tax to "centimillionaires" (wealth above $100M, ~50,000 people globally) would raise an additional **$100–140 billion per year**.
- Total potential revenue: $300–390 billion per year — comparable in magnitude to all foreign aid combined and roughly 2× what climate-finance pledges have committed.

The political trajectory:

- **2024 G20 (Brazilian presidency)**: Endorsed in principle in the July 2024 finance ministers' communiqué.
- **2025 G20 (South African presidency)**: Continued political support; technical work commissioned.
- **2026 G20 (U.S. presidency)**: Substantive progress paused under the Trump administration's announced opposition.
- **EU**: Active discussion; Spain has championed; France has been supportive but has not pushed.

The proposal is in roughly the position that Pillar Two was in around 2018–2019: agreed in principle by a substantial coalition, technically well-developed, but waiting for political conditions to make multilateral signature feasible. The same dynamic that produced Pillar Two (sufficient revenue gain that even rule-following countries net positive, combined with Global South pressure) could plausibly produce billionaire-minimum-tax adoption on a 5–10 year horizon — but the U.S. exit from Pillar Two cooperation in 2026 is a significant headwind.

## The Global Justice Fund: the maximal version of the same logic

The billionaire minimum tax is a floor. In June 2026 the World Inequality Lab published the [Global Justice Report](https://globaljusticeproject.wid.world/global-justice-report/), which asks what the same coordination machinery would look like if it were sized to the problem rather than to current political tolerance. Its centrepiece is a **[Global Justice Fund](https://globaljusticeproject.wid.world/insight/chapter-2/)** running from 2026 to 2100, financed by a global wealth tax and a global income tax, with revenues either paid out as equal per-capita country dividends or accumulated in a World Sovereign Fund.

The numbers are an order of magnitude beyond anything else on this page. The wealth tax rises from 0% at ten times average world wealth (about €1.1 million in 2026) to 20% a year above roughly €550 million, so every billionaire pays an effective 20% — ten times Zucman's floor. The income tax reaches 90% at the very top. Together they touch about 1% of the world's population and raise, in the 2026–2035 decade, 6.8% and 4.0% of world GDP respectively. Total fund flows average 8–10% of world GDP over 2026–2060, roughly €12–14 trillion in 2026 alone, against current development aid of under 0.4%.

Two features make this more than a wish list. The first is that the fund is designed to shrink its own tax base: wealth-tax revenue falls from 6.8% of world GDP to 1.0% by 2050 as concentration compresses, and by 2100 the entire revenue base is investment income from the World Sovereign Fund, which accumulates to about 60% of world GDP. The second is that the report confronts the defection problem the rest of this page treats as decisive, proposing corrective tariffs on non-participants — around 80% on US exports, 180% or more on China's. Whether a coalition capable of levying those tariffs could form is precisely the question, and the report does not settle it.

The distributional claim is that 85–95% of the Global North and 95–98% of the Global South end up better off once leisure and environmental preservation are valued, with headline targets of a 1-to-5 global income scale, the bottom 50% share of wealth rising from 2% to 30%, and warming held to 1.8°C. Those projections rest on a modelled century-long path, and a century is long enough that the growth, demographic, and political assumptions inside it are doing a great deal of work. The report is best read not as a forecast but as an existence proof: it establishes that the arithmetic of financing global convergence closes, which is a different and weaker claim than showing it could be enacted. Its governance chapter is the more immediately usable contribution, documenting that Europe and North America hold roughly four times as many IMF and World Bank votes as their population share while Sub-Saharan Africa and South and South-East Asia hold four times fewer, and proposing decisions by 55% of countries representing 60% of world population as the transitional rule.

We treat this as the outer bound of the coordination agenda rather than a near-term option. The 2% floor has a G20 communiqué behind it and a plausible 5–10 year path; a 20% billionaire rate has neither. But the report is useful for locating the ceiling, and its reparations arithmetic is worth carrying separately: it estimates colonial and climate obligations at about 3% of world GDP annually against the 0.8% its own North-South transfers deliver, which is an unusually concrete statement of a gap normally argued in qualitative terms.

## Tax havens 2.0: from offshore banking to offshore profit-shifting

The 2010s transparency reforms (CRS, FATCA, beneficial-ownership registries) substantially reduced the appeal of traditional offshore banking — hiding personal wealth in numbered Swiss accounts. The flow of hidden personal wealth has not disappeared but has shifted toward two newer channels:

1. **Real estate** (covered in [Financial Regulation](/interventions/financial-regulation/)).
2. **Corporate profit shifting** by multinationals — using transfer pricing, intellectual property licensing, and conduit subsidiaries to relocate paper profits to low-tax jurisdictions.

Corporate profit shifting is now the dominant form of "tax haven" usage by dollar magnitude. Estimates from [Tørsløv, Wier & Zucman (2023, *RES*, "The Missing Profits of Nations")](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/90/3/1499/6888397) put global profit shifting at roughly **$600 billion per year** (2015) — about 36% of all corporate profits earned outside their parent country are shifted to tax havens. The largest haven-recipients are Ireland, Singapore, Switzerland, the Netherlands, and a small number of Caribbean and Asian jurisdictions; the largest losers are large continental European countries (Germany, France, Italy) and the U.S.

This is what Pillar Two was designed to address. By imposing a 15% effective floor, Pillar Two reduces the *return* on profit shifting: even if a multinational successfully relocates profits to a 5%-tax jurisdiction, the parent country (or another signatory) collects the 10% top-up. The empirical question is whether 15% is high enough to substantially curb the practice — early indications are mixed, with some shifting reduction visible in 2024–2025 data but with substantial residual flows continuing.

The next-generation reform discussion has focused on **raising the Pillar Two minimum to 20–25%** and on **closing the residual loopholes** (substance-based income exclusions, R&D allowances) that currently make the 15% effective rate substantially lower in practice. Both reforms are in early-stage discussion, principally at the EU and at the UN Tax Convention.

## Interaction with financial regulation and progressive taxation

The interventions on this page are infrastructural for the substantive interventions in [Progressive Taxation](/interventions/progressive-taxation/) and [Financial Regulation](/interventions/financial-regulation/). The relationship is asymmetric: progressive taxation can exist without international coordination (most countries operate progressive income and wealth taxes domestically), but the *rates* and *effective base* of those taxes are constrained by international coordination. A country with strong domestic tax administration and weak international coordination faces a much higher elasticity of taxable income than a country with both.

The empirical record supports this. The countries that have most successfully maintained meaningful corporate, capital-gains, and wealth taxation since 1980 (Norway, the Netherlands before its tax-haven shift, Switzerland in some respects) have done so primarily through domestic enforcement quality. The countries that have most successfully *raised* effective tax rates in the 2020s (those implementing Pillar Two) have done so through international coordination. The two are complementary rather than substitute.

For the wealth-and-power agenda specifically, the priority order is approximately:

1. **Beneficial-ownership transparency and CRS expansion** (low political cost, high revenue impact).
2. **Pillar Two implementation and enforcement** (already in motion; preserving and extending what exists).
3. **Billionaire minimum tax** (high political cost, very high revenue impact).
4. **Pillar One / digital reallocation** (currently stalled; revisit when political conditions permit).
5. **UN Tax Convention** (long-horizon institutional reform; matters more for the next 20 years than the next 5).

Each rung supports the next: beneficial-ownership data makes Pillar Two more enforceable; Pillar Two infrastructure makes a billionaire minimum tax operationally feasible; UN Convention provides the multilateral architecture for both. This is the make-wealth-visible pillar of the [Synthesis: Core Strategy](/synthesis/core-strategy/) — necessarily international because the wealth itself is international.

## Objections it must clear

International coordination exists to answer the strongest version of the [cure-is-worse objection](/current-state/justifications-for-inequality/#the-cure-is-worse-than-the-disease): that taxing mobile capital simply drives it offshore, so the attempt is self-defeating and triggers a destructive race to the bottom. Coordination is the response that takes the objection seriously — it removes the exit option the objection relies on. A unilateral wealth or corporate tax can be arbitraged by relocation; a coordinated minimum (the OECD Pillar Two floor, automatic exchange of bank information, beneficial-ownership registries) raises the cost of the arbitrage itself. The [overstated-numbers objection](/current-state/justifications-for-inequality/#the-numbers-are-overstated) also leans partly on offshore wealth being unmeasured — an argument that cuts toward transparency, not away from it, since the same registries that enable taxation close the measurement gap.

## Distributional effects: gender and race/ethnicity

The distributional stakes of coordination are clearest in two places. Domestically, offshore and untaxed capital sits at the very top of the wealth distribution, which is disproportionately white and male; recovering it shifts incidence onto that group for the same structural reason as estate taxation. Internationally, the revenue at stake funds public services on which women — as the majority of unpaid carers and of public-sector and frontline workers — disproportionately depend, so erosion of the corporate and capital tax base carries a gendered cost borne most heavily in lower-income countries ([OECD 2022, *Tax Policy and Gender Equality*](https://www.oecd-ilibrary.org/taxation/tax-policy-and-gender-equality_b8177abb-en); [Tax Justice Network gender work](https://www.taxjustice.net/reports/)). The evidence base here is thinner and more global than for the domestic interventions, and the gendered-incidence claim rests on the composition of public spending rather than on direct tax-by-gender data, a limitation worth flagging rather than overstating. Where the erosion bites, it bites unevenly within the group it names. The public health, water, and schooling that a starved tax base cuts first are relied on most by rural, Indigenous, and migrant women, who hold the fewest private substitutes, so the loss concentrates at the intersection of gender with the local hierarchy of ethnicity or citizenship. The [comprehensive strategy](/synthesis/core-strategy/) frames that intersectional test.
