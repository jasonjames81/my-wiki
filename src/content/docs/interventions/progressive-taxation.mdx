title: Progressive Taxation
description: Income, wealth, inheritance, and capital-gains taxation as primary levers for redistributing concentrated wealth, with evidence on each.
---

Progressive taxation is the most studied and most directly effective intervention for reducing post-tax wealth concentration. It operates on three margins: redistributing existing wealth (inheritance and wealth taxes), redistributing flow income (progressive income tax), and reducing the rate of capital accumulation at the top (capital gains and corporate taxation). Each margin has substantial empirical literature, summarized below.

The strongest evidence — both in effect size and in identification — is for **inheritance and estate taxation**. Cross-country studies (Piketty, Saez & Zucman 2014; OECD 2021) show that countries with effective inheritance taxes have meaningfully lower wealth concentration even controlling for income tax progressivity. The weakest evidence is for net **wealth taxes**, which have been adopted and then largely abandoned across Europe due to administrative costs and capital flight; the recent revival proposals (Saez & Zucman 2019; Scheuer & Slemrod 2021) address most of these design flaws but lack long-run real-world data.

Behavioral responses (avoidance, evasion, migration) are the central empirical battleground. Top-end elasticities range from near zero (e.g., U.S. estate tax, Kopczuk 2013) to large (e.g., 1990s Scandinavian wealth taxes, Jakobsen et al. 2020). Design matters enormously: enforcement, third-party reporting, and international coordination determine whether a given rate is real or nominal.

## Effectiveness summary

| Instrument                | Direct effect on top shares | Behavioral elasticity (well-designed) | Implementation difficulty |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Inheritance / estate tax  | Large, persistent           | Low–moderate                          | Medium                    |
| Progressive income tax    | Moderate                    | Moderate                              | Low (already in place)    |
| Capital gains @ ordinary rates | Moderate–large         | Moderate (lock-in effects)            | Low–medium                |
| Net wealth tax            | Large in theory             | High under poor design                | High (valuation, evasion) |
| Corporate income tax      | Moderate (incidence debated)| Moderate                              | Medium                    |
| Minimum global corp. tax  | Moderate                    | Low (eliminates arbitrage)            | High (coordination)       |

## The elasticity of taxable income literature

The single most important parameter in optimal-tax analysis is the **elasticity of taxable income (ETI)** with respect to the net-of-tax rate — that is, by what percentage does reported taxable income fall when the after-tax share of an additional dollar of earnings falls by one percent? Higher elasticities mean that a tax increase produces more behavioral response (less work, more avoidance, more migration, more shifting into untaxed forms) and therefore raises less revenue and creates more distortion than a naive static analysis would suggest.

The canonical review is [Saez, Slemrod & Giertz (2012, *JEL*, "The Elasticity of Taxable Income with Respect to Marginal Tax Rates: A Critical Review")](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjel.50.1.3). The 50-year-old literature it surveys reaches three durable conclusions:

1. **ETI is genuinely positive but smaller than the popular debate assumes.** Best-quasi-experimental estimates for the U.S. cluster around 0.2–0.4 for top earners, with substantial variation across studies and time periods.
2. **Most of the response is *avoidance*, not *real* economic adjustment.** Top earners shift income across periods, recharacterize compensation, accelerate or delay realization of capital gains, and (where possible) shift income to lower-tax jurisdictions. They do not measurably reduce work effort or capital investment in the magnitudes that would imply large efficiency costs.
3. **ETI is highly *policy-elastic*.** Eliminating an avoidance opportunity (e.g., closing a deduction, requiring third-party reporting, harmonizing tax bases internationally) substantially reduces the measured ETI for the same statutory rate. The rate is not the binding constraint; the design and enforcement are.

The third conclusion is what makes the whole tax-design literature actionable. A 50% top rate with porous rules and weak enforcement raises less revenue and produces more avoidance than a 40% rate with airtight design. The *Tax Cuts and Jobs Act* opened or widened multiple avoidance channels (Section 199A pass-through deduction, GILTI design choices, opportunity zones); subsequent reform proposals to close these channels operate on the design margin rather than the rate margin.

## Why estate taxes have low elasticity

The popular intuition is that estate taxes face the highest behavioral response because they fall on the wealthy at the moment of greatest planning sophistication and the assets are highly mobile. The empirical record runs strongly the other way.

[Kopczuk (2013, NBER WP 15741, "Economics of Estate Taxation")](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15741/w15741.pdf) reviews the literature and estimates the long-run elasticity of *reported* estates with respect to the net-of-tax rate at **0.1–0.2** — roughly half the magnitude of top-end income ETI and small enough that estate taxation is approximately revenue-maximizing across a wide range of statutory rates. Three structural features explain the low elasticity:

1. **Death is exogenous.** Unlike income, which can be retimed, recharacterized, or relocated, the *event* of estate transfer cannot be deferred indefinitely. Avoidance has to operate on *who* the heir is and *what* form the asset takes, not on whether the transfer happens.
2. **Most estate-tax avoidance is structural, not behavioral.** Trusts, GRATs (grantor-retained annuity trusts), valuation discounts, and charitable bequests reduce *measured* taxable estates without reducing *real* wealth transfer. Closing these structural channels reduces measured elasticity directly.
3. **Estate taxation interacts with bequest motives, not work motives.** Since most estate holders are post-retirement, the tax does not measurably reduce labor supply. The "deathbed planning" Kopczuk documents — disposal of business assets and collectibles in the weeks before death — is real but small in aggregate.

The implication is that estate taxation is one of the most *under*-utilized progressive instruments. The U.S. estate tax exemption rose from $675K in 2001 to $13.6M per individual ($27.2M per married couple) in 2024 — effectively turning the tax from a broad-based instrument into one that affects only the top ~0.1% of estates. Restoring a meaningful estate tax (lower exemption, fewer structural loopholes) is the highest-evidence-to-effect intervention in this section.

## The European wealth-tax retreats

The cautionary tale for ambitious wealth taxation is that **most European countries that adopted broad-based annual wealth taxes in the 1980s have since abandoned them**. France abolished its *impôt de solidarité sur la fortune* in 2018; Germany suspended its wealth tax in 1997 (constitutional ruling on valuation); Sweden abolished in 2007; Denmark abolished in 1997; Austria, Finland, and Ireland abolished earlier. Only Norway, Spain, and Switzerland retain meaningful wealth taxes today.

The most rigorous analysis of *why* is [Jakobsen, Jakobsen, Kleven & Zucman (2020, *QJE*, "Wealth Taxation and Wealth Accumulation: Theory and Evidence from Denmark")](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/135/1/329/5584349). Using Danish administrative wealth records and the 1989 wealth-tax reform as a quasi-experiment, they find:

- The **long-run elasticity of wealth with respect to the net-of-tax rate of return is ~0.7** — substantially higher than the elasticity of *income* with respect to the net-of-tax rate.
- Effects compound over time: 30 years after a tax cut, wealth is ~30% higher than it would have been under the original tax regime.
- The bulk of the response is **changes in saving behavior**, not avoidance/evasion — wealthy households respond to higher net returns by saving more.

The Danish elasticity is 3–4 times the estate-tax elasticity Kopczuk documents. The reason is that wealth taxes *recur annually*, so the avoidance horizon is much longer and the cumulative effect compounds. The retreats from European wealth taxes were also driven by:

- **Valuation problems** (illiquid assets, private business stakes, art) that produced both administrative cost and political controversy.
- **Capital flight to lower-tax jurisdictions**, especially before the Common Reporting Standard made cross-border wealth visible.
- **Selective coverage** — most European wealth taxes exempted business assets and primary residences, which are the bulk of upper-middle-class wealth and the bulk of *productive* wealth at the top, leaving the tax base disproportionately financial-asset-rich households.

[Saez & Zucman (2019, *BPEA*)](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Saez-Zucman_conference-draft.pdf) and [Scheuer & Slemrod (2021, *JEP*, "Taxing Our Wealth")](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28150/w28150.pdf) develop modern wealth-tax design proposals that respond to these failures. Key features include: high exemption thresholds (only top 0.1% or 0.01%), broad asset coverage, mark-to-market valuation for traded assets, formula-based valuation for non-traded business interests, exit-tax provisions for emigration, and most importantly, *international coordination* (see [Global Tax Coordination](/interventions/global-tax-coordination/)) to prevent the cross-border leakage that doomed earlier designs.

## Capital gains reform: realization vs. mark-to-market

Capital gains taxation is among the largest sources of effective progressivity at the very top, because the very top of the wealth distribution holds disproportionately appreciable assets and realizes income largely through capital gains rather than wages. The U.S. system taxes capital gains at preferential rates (long-term gains at 0/15/20% vs. ordinary income at up to 37%) and only at *realization* (sale of the asset). Both features are politically contested.

The realization rule produces three well-documented distortions:

1. **Lock-in.** Investors hold appreciated assets longer than optimal to defer the tax, distorting portfolio allocation and reducing market efficiency.
2. **Step-up at death.** Under U.S. law, heirs receive a "stepped-up basis" on inherited assets — capital gains accrued during the decedent's lifetime are *never* taxed. This combined with the estate tax exemption means that a substantial fraction of top-end wealth permanently escapes capital gains taxation.
3. **Borrow against appreciated assets.** Wealthy holders can borrow at low rates against appreciated stock, consuming the proceeds without realizing the gain — the "buy, borrow, die" strategy. This is the dominant mechanism by which billionaires fund consumption while reporting low taxable income.

The reform proposals operate on three margins:

| Reform                                  | Mechanism                                   | Behavioral elasticity      | Revenue potential |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ----------------- |
| Tax capital gains at ordinary rates     | Eliminate the preferential 20% top rate     | Moderate (lock-in worsens) | Moderate          |
| Tax gains at death (eliminate step-up)  | Treat death as a realization event          | Low                        | Moderate–large    |
| Mark-to-market for liquid assets        | Tax annual appreciation regardless of sale  | Low (for liquid assets)    | Large             |
| Billionaire minimum tax (Wyden / Biden) | 20–25% minimum on income including unrealized gains for ultra-wealthy | Low (narrow base) | Moderate |

The Biden 2022/2024 budget proposals, the Wyden 2021 mark-to-market proposal, and the Zucman billionaire minimum tax proposal all target some combination of step-up elimination, mark-to-market, and minimum-tax floors. None has been enacted, but they represent the live policy frontier on capital-gains progressivity.

## Interaction with international coordination

The single largest constraint on domestic progressive taxation is the threat of capital and tax-base flight to lower-tax jurisdictions. This constraint is not absolute — empirical evidence (see [Kleven et al.](https://www.henrikkleven.com/research/working_papers/WealthMig_Nov2025.pdf)) suggests that high-end migration responses are real but smaller than commonly claimed — but it is meaningful enough that *unilateral* progressive tax reforms face a much higher elasticity than *coordinated* ones would.

The OECD Pillar Two global minimum corporate tax (15%, in force in the EU/U.K./Japan/Korea since 2024–25) is the working proof of concept. By coordinating internationally, signatory countries reduce the credibility of profit-shifting threats and can sustain higher effective rates than any one of them could unilaterally. The Zucman billionaire-minimum-tax proposal endorsed at the 2024 G20 ministerial extends this logic to personal wealth.

[Global Tax Coordination](/interventions/global-tax-coordination/) develops this in detail. The summary for purposes of progressive taxation: the highest-evidence interventions on this page (estate tax, capital gains at ordinary rates, mark-to-market for the very top) all face elasticity floors set by international coordination. Restoring those floors makes the *same* statutory rates much more effective.
