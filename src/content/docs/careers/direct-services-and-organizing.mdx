Organizing and direct-services roles supply the grassroots power that everything else in this wiki depends on. They are the natural fit for candidates who want to build movements rather than analyze or fund them, and they are the most credentialing-agnostic of all the career categories surveyed here — what matters is field experience, relationships, and demonstrated capacity to move groups of people.

The roles fall into three loose clusters. **Labor organizing** roles (union organizer, business agent, research staff embedded in labor) build worker power inside firms and industries; the major employers are unions (SEIU, CWA, UAW, AFL-CIO, sectoral unions in Europe) and union-aligned research/training institutes. **Community and tenant organizing** roles (community organizer, lead organizer, executive director of a community organization) build geographic power; the major employers are IAF affiliates, PICO/Faith in Action, Center for Popular Democracy affiliates, ACORN successors, tenant-union federations. **Nonprofit operations** — finance director, executive director, COO of a community or movement organization — are where accounting and finance backgrounds add unusual value to organizations that often run lean and credential-light.

For candidates moving from corporate or professional careers, the most common landing spots are operational and finance leadership in mid-sized movement organizations (where strong financial management can dramatically expand an organization's effectiveness) and policy/research roles inside unions (where economics and accounting credentials are valued and underrepresented).

## Organizing as a career: paths, pay, and durability

Organizing is a genuine profession with a career ladder, but it is also a profession with high turnover, demanding conditions, and compensation well below private-sector equivalents. Understanding the realistic career arc matters for candidates considering the path.

**Union organizing** typically begins with an entry-level organizer position at a union (typically $45,000–$60,000 + expenses and benefits). The role involves extensive travel, irregular hours, and direct relationship-building with workers at targeted workplaces or in targeted industries. First-year organizers are typically assigned to campaigns led by more experienced staff; after 2–4 years, successful organizers take on lead organizer roles and begin managing campaigns and other organizers. The career ladder runs: organizer → lead organizer → regional director → political/field director → national staff leadership.

The major U.S. unions with the most active new-organizing programs: **SEIU** (healthcare, property services; runs one of the largest training infrastructures for new organizers); **CWA** (communications workers and the Campaign to Organize Digital Employees, CODE-CWA); **UAW** (auto and academic workers; [2024 win at Volkswagen Chattanooga](https://michiganadvance.com/2024/04/20/following-the-uaws-wins-against-the-detroit-three-tenn-volkswagen-workers-vote-to-join-the-union/) and ongoing battery-plant campaigns, though a concurrent campaign at Mercedes-Benz Alabama was lost); **UNITE HERE** (hospitality; known for intensive house-calling methodology); **Teamsters** (logistics; recent organizing wins at Amazon and affiliation of the ALU).

The compensation trade-off is real: union organizers are paid significantly below what their education and skill level would earn in the private sector, and the demanding conditions produce substantial burnout. The organizing community has developed significant support infrastructure — training programs, health benefits, and (in some unions) a culture that treats organizers as professionals rather than expendables — but the structural tension between mission and personal sustainability is genuine.

**Community and tenant organizing** pays even less than union organizing in most contexts. Entry-level community organizer salaries at IAF or PICO affiliates are typically $38,000–$55,000. The career ladder is shallower: organizer → lead organizer → executive director of a community organization is the most common trajectory. The executive director of a mid-sized community organization might earn $80,000–$130,000; at large organizations (National People's Action affiliates, Center for Popular Democracy), higher. IAF's model is unusual in that it pays senior organizers relatively well and invests substantially in training, producing a more professionalized career path than many community-organizing traditions.

**Durability**: the honest assessment is that organizing careers are concentrated in the 25–45 age range and involve significant self-selection. Organizers who stay in the field for 15+ years typically move into organizational leadership or training/education roles. The skills are genuinely transferable — labor organizing experience is highly valued in advocacy, political campaigns, and increasingly in tech-sector and foundation roles.

## The training pipelines

The major organizing traditions each have distinct training infrastructure.

**[Industrial Areas Foundation (IAF)](https://www.industrialareasfoundation.org/)**: The oldest and most formalized U.S. community-organizing tradition, founded by Saul Alinsky and continued through BUILD, COPS, and other metropolitan organizations. IAF trains through its annual National Training (an intensive 8–10 day residential program, alternating between Chicago and Los Angeles) and regional 5-day trainings, plus a 90-day organizer internship. IAF organizing is relationship-based and congregation-centered (most affiliates are faith-based); the theory of change emphasizes building relational power through one-on-one meetings before deploying it in public actions. IAF affiliates in major cities — Chicago (UNO, BUILD), Los Angeles (LAANE), New York (East Brooklyn Congregations), Texas (COPS, METRO Alliance) — are the entry points.

**PICO / [Faith in Action](https://faithinaction.org/)**: Similar to IAF in its congregation-centered approach; more explicitly multi-faith and increasingly engaged in national campaigns beyond the city level. Faith in Action (rebranded from PICO National Network in 2018) runs quarterly national leadership trainings of roughly six days; regional networks provide apprenticeship infrastructure. National campaigns on immigration, healthcare, and criminal justice have given Faith in Action/PICO organizers more exposure to federal-level policy than IAF's locally-focused model.

**Center for Popular Democracy (CPD)**: A federation of state and local organizations with more explicit political orientation than IAF or PICO. CPD affiliates (Make the Road New York, VOCAL-NY, Florida Rights Restoration Coalition, others) engage in both grassroots organizing and direct electoral work. Training is less formalized than IAF; mentorship within affiliate organizations is the primary pipeline.

**Labor organizing training**: The major unions' training programs include the [AFL-CIO's Organizing Institute](https://aflcio.org/about-us/programs/organizing-institute) (a 3-day skills training that serves as the gateway to a 12-week paid Campaign Apprenticeship, followed by a 9-month AFL-CIO organizing fellowship; participating unions set starting salaries beginning around $55,000), SEIU's extensive internal training infrastructure, and university-affiliated labor-studies programs (Cornell ILR, Michigan IRLE, Harvard Labor and Worklife Program, UCLA IRLE). The Organizing Institute in particular is the major structured entry point for people without existing union connections who want to become union organizers.

## Why mid-career finance and accounting professionals are unusually valuable

Movement organizations are run chronically lean on operational and financial capacity. The result is an organizational vulnerability that mid-career professionals from finance and accounting can address directly.

The specific deficits are consistent across the sector:
- **Financial management** that lags the organization's growth (organizations that have grown from $500K to $5M budgets while operating with $500K-era accounting infrastructure).
- **Compliance gaps** — 990 preparation, audit processes, multi-state registration for national organizations, federal grant compliance — that absorb disproportionate leadership attention.
- **Cash-flow management** failures, particularly in grant-dependent organizations where grants arrive unevenly and programs need to continue between grants.
- **Budget-to-actual reporting** that is too late and too aggregate to be useful for program directors managing expenditures in real time.
- **Investment and reserve management** at organizations large enough to have endowments or operating reserves but that haven't built investment committee infrastructure.

A CPA or experienced finance professional who joins a mid-sized movement organization as finance director or CFO is not doing less valuable work than if they joined a think tank or government agency — they are providing organizational capacity that is genuinely scarce in the sector and that determines whether the organization can sustain its programs over time.

The practical path: most mid-sized movement organizations ($3–20M annual budget) advertise finance director or CFO positions through idealist.org, progressiverecruiters.com, and their network of peer organizations. The credential requirements are often lower than equivalent positions in the private sector (organizations that need a CPA often advertise for anyone with a bachelor's in accounting and 5 years of experience), and the compensation is below private-sector equivalents — but the functional impact is higher because the organization's management capacity is the binding constraint on its effectiveness.

**Nonprofit financial certification**: The [Nonprofit Finance Fund (NFF)](https://nff.org/learn) provides webinar series and advisory programs for nonprofit financial staff that bridge the gap between standard accounting credentials and nonprofit-specific requirements (grant accounting, restricted-fund management, FASB ASC 958). The Fiscal Management Associates (FMA) nonprofit provides similar capacity-building consulting. Candidates from for-profit accounting backgrounds often find these resources useful for translating their skills to the nonprofit context.

## Worker cooperatives and ESOP advising as a finance-adjacent path

Two adjacent roles are worth noting for candidates with finance or accounting backgrounds who want to work on the predistribution agenda directly.

**Worker cooperative development**: The [predistribution agenda](/interventions/predistribution/) includes significant emphasis on broadening asset ownership through worker cooperatives and employee ownership. The organizations that support cooperative development — U.S. Federation of Worker Cooperatives (USFWC), [National Center for Employee Ownership (NCEO)](https://www.nceo.org/), [Democracy at Work Institute (DAWI)](https://institute.coop/), and regional cooperative development centers — employ staff who combine finance and legal knowledge with organizational development skills. The work involves: business planning and feasibility analysis for new cooperatives; governance design (the internal democratic structure of a cooperative is complex); financing (cooperative loan funds, CDFI participation); and ongoing technical assistance to operating cooperatives. CPA and finance professionals who understand business valuation, loan underwriting, and operational finance are genuinely useful in these roles.

**ESOP conversion advising**: Employee Stock Ownership Plans are a tax-advantaged mechanism for converting privately held businesses to employee ownership. ESOPs are technically complex — the transaction structure involves a leveraged buyout of the founding owners by a trust that holds shares on behalf of employees — and require specialized legal (ERISA counsel), financial (ESOP feasibility and valuation), and organizational development (governance and culture) expertise. The advisor community includes: **[Project Equity](https://project-equity.org/)** (nonprofit conversion advisor and capacity builder); **Ownership Works** (supports ESOP adoption in private-equity-backed firms); **[National Center for Employee Ownership](https://www.nceo.org/)** (education and research); and numerous private ESOP trustees, attorneys, and advisors. A CPA with experience in business valuation or M&A has directly applicable skills for ESOP advisory work. The ESOP Association and NCEO both offer certification programs and professional communities.

## How organizing roles interact with the broader career map

Organizing experience is unusually transferable to adjacent career paths in ways that credentials-focused career paths are not.

**Organizing → Advocacy**: Lead organizers who have run multi-site campaigns frequently move into campaign-director or coalition-coordinator roles at advocacy organizations. The skills transfer directly: managing coalitions, identifying and moving decision-makers, sustaining campaigns through setbacks. ATF, SEIU's political department, and most major advocacy organizations have significant staff from organizing backgrounds.

**Organizing → Electoral politics**: Field organizers are the primary labor supply for electoral campaigns. The skills are nearly identical — voter contact instead of worker contact; candidate instead of union as the object of mobilization — and organizing alumni are disproportionately present in Democratic campaign staff and in the consultancy firms that support those campaigns. The entry points are field director roles in congressional or state-level campaigns; the Analyst Institute and leadership academies associated with the AFL-CIO run structured programs for labor organizers entering electoral work.

**Organizing → Research**: Union research staff positions are a distinct but adjacent path for organizers who develop strong analytical skills. These roles involve contract costing (analyzing management proposals to quantify their economic effects), corporate research (Dun & Bradstreet analysis, SEC filings, comparative compensation research), and technical analysis of health-insurance and retirement proposals. The AFL-CIO's research department and SEIU's research team are the largest employers; major international unions (UAW, UFCW, UNITE HERE) all have in-house research capacity. Organizers who develop research skills — often self-taught and applied to live campaigns — are the primary pipeline.

**Nonprofit operations → Board service**: Finance directors and COOs at movement organizations frequently transition to board service at peer organizations after retiring from staff roles. Board service provides continued influence on organizational direction without the operational demands of staff work. For mid-career professionals from finance backgrounds who want continued engagement with the sector after retirement or career transition, board roles at movement organizations and CDFIs are a common path.

ESOP advisor private-sector range is indicative.

The compensation picture is the starkest of any career path in this wiki: a labor organizer earns roughly what a first-year associate at a large law firm earns in a month. This is not primarily a market-clearing wage — it is a sector-wide pay structure that reflects the historical weakness of organizing as an institution and the assumption that movement work comes with a mission premium. The premium is real for some candidates; for others, especially those with significant student-loan debt or family financial responsibilities, it creates a genuine access barrier that limits the sector's talent pool. Loan-forgiveness programs ([PSLF for nonprofit employees](https://studentaid.gov/manage-loans/forgiveness-cancellation/public-service)) and union benefits partially offset the gap; for many candidates, they do not.
