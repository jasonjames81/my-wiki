Philanthropy roles sit at an awkward but consequential point in the field. Foundations are themselves products of historic wealth concentration (Ford, Rockefeller, Hewlett, Open Society, more recently Omidyar, Arnold, Hutchins) and yet they are the primary funders of much of the research and advocacy infrastructure cataloged in [Section 4](/organizations/research-policy-institutes/). A program officer at a foundation focused on economic inequality can move tens of millions of dollars per year toward the interventions in this wiki — sometimes more than they could from inside a research or advocacy organization directly.

The structural ambivalence is real and worth naming: philanthropy directs resources but does so through the priorities of the donor class, which produces a known set of biases (toward technocratic interventions, away from labor and grassroots organizing, toward measurable short-horizon outcomes). The most effective program officers acknowledge this tension and actively counterweight it — supporting capacity-building, general operating support, and grassroots organizations that are harder to fund through more conventional pathways.

Adjacent roles include **intermediaries** (Tides, Borealis, NEO Philanthropy, Solidaire) that re-grant donor funds with more progressive infrastructure; **donor-organizing organizations** (Patriotic Millionaires for political advocacy, Resource Generation for next-gen wealthy donors); and **wealth-management for movement work** (Threshold Foundation, Hawthorn Capital members, family-office staff focused on impact). For CPAs and finance professionals, family-office and intermediary roles can be unusually high-leverage.

## The major foundations active in inequality work

The foundations funding inequality-relevant research and advocacy are not homogeneous. They vary in size, governance model, thematic priorities, and willingness to fund contentious advocacy. Understanding the differences is essential for roles within them and for the organizations they fund.

**Ford Foundation** is the largest single funder of progressive policy work in the United States, with approximately $16B in endowment (total assets of $17.5B as of its [2024 audited financial statements](https://www.fordfoundation.org/about/about-ford/governance-and-financial-statements/financial-statements/2024-audited-financial-statements-and-footnotes/)) and annual grantmaking of roughly $840M in 2024. Its *Future of Work(ers)* initiative covers labor organizing, worker power, and wage policy; its *Civic Engagement and Government* work covers democratic reforms and anti-corruption; its *Inclusive Economies* work covers inequality frameworks. Ford explicitly funds general operating support — the most valuable form of philanthropic support for advocacy organizations, which prefer unrestricted funds. Ford's program officers are highly sought-after: the organization is well-staffed, pays market-rate salaries, and has the prestige that opens doors.

**Open Society Foundations** (OSF) was the largest single global funder of progressive civil society until its [2023 restructuring](https://ssir.org/articles/entry/open_society_under_threat), which consolidated from a roughly $1.5B/year operation — its endowment now stands at approximately $23B — with significant staff reductions (at least 40% of international staff) and a shift in grantmaking structure. As of 2024, OSF's total expenditures were approximately $1.2B with roughly $700M disbursed as grants, per its [published financial figures](https://www.opensocietyfoundations.org/who-we-are/financials). The restructuring substantially reduced European program budgets; program officers in affected areas were displaced. OSF's model (historically very relationship-driven, with large discretionary grants to trusted organizations) distinguishes it from more process-heavy foundation grantmaking.

**Hewlett Foundation** is a roughly $14B endowment foundation (approximately $13.9B in assets as of December 31, 2024, per its [financials page](https://hewlett.org/about-us/financials/)) with major programs in economy and society (including economic growth and opportunity), environment, and education. In 2024, Hewlett awarded $631M in grants, nearly three-fourths as flexible funding. Its economy work is more technocratic than Ford's or OSF's — Hewlett tends to fund research institutes and policy design over advocacy organizations. The William and Flora Hewlett Foundation has been a significant funder of WIL/WID, WCEG, and academic economists working on inequality.

**Arnold Ventures** (formerly the Laura and John Arnold Foundation, [restructured as an LLC in 2019](https://www.insidephilanthropy.com/home/2019-1-28-when-philanthropy-is-not-enough-a-top-billionaire-donor-couple-take-a-broader-approach-to-impact)) is distinct from traditional foundation grantmaking in that it is a limited liability company that wraps both a 501(c)(3) foundation and a 501(c)(4) advocacy arm, giving it more flexibility to fund nonprofit and advocacy activities than a pure private foundation. The foundation component had assets of approximately $4.8B as of 2024. Its criminal justice reform work is its largest program; its inequality-relevant work includes evidence-based policy research and some labor-market work. Arnold tends to fund evidence-based interventions with measurable outcomes, which creates a known bias against harder-to-measure advocacy and organizing.

**Omidyar Network** (Pierre Omidyar's investment vehicle) operates across social impact investing and philanthropy. Its work touches inequality through democracy and technology policy (Luminate), as well as direct giving to advocacy and research organizations. Like Arnold, Omidyar Network uses a [hybrid LLC and 501(c)(3) structure](https://www.insidephilanthropy.com/home/these-6-major-funders-are-llcs-not-foundations) rather than a purely traditional private foundation, providing flexibility to support both nonprofits and mission-aligned businesses.

**Rockefeller Brothers Fund and Rockefeller Foundation** (distinct entities) have historical prestige exceeding their current endowment size. The [Rockefeller Brothers Fund](https://www.rbf.org/about/transparency/annual-reviews) (~$1.35B endowment as of its 2024 annual review, up nearly 50% over the past decade) focuses on democratic practice and sustainable development; the [Rockefeller Foundation](https://www.rockefellerfoundation.org/governance-reports/) (~$6.4B as of its 2024 financial statements) focuses on health, food, and energy.

**Smaller and newer funders** active in the inequality space include: the Kresge Foundation (community development, place-based inequality); the JPB Foundation (poverty, health, environment); the Marguerite Casey Foundation (grassroots organizations for low-income communities); Wellspring Philanthropic Fund (donor-advised fund that backs progressive advocacy); and the Berger Action Fund (explicit 501(c)(4) grantmaking for legislative advocacy).

## The program-officer role in detail

A program officer at a major foundation manages a portfolio of grants (typically $30–80M for a full program officer, larger for senior program officers) toward a defined strategic goal. The day-to-day work:

**Grant sourcing and due diligence.** Program officers identify grantee organizations through field networks, unsolicited proposals, and proactive outreach. Due diligence involves organizational assessment (financial health, governance, track record, leadership), programmatic fit (does this grant advance the foundation's theory of change?), and sometimes site visits and reference calls. At process-heavy foundations (Ford, Hewlett), due diligence is formalized and involves substantial documentation; at relationship-driven foundations, informal trust plays a larger role.

**Grant approval.** Grants above a threshold (varies by foundation; typically $100K–$500K) require program-director or board approval. Program officers write grant memoranda justifying the grant to internal reviewers. The quality of grant memos is often the most visible measure of a program officer's effectiveness to senior leadership.

**Portfolio management.** Once grants are made, program officers monitor grantees' progress, review financial reports, participate in site visits, and manage renewals. The ongoing relationship between program officer and grantee is the core operational relationship in philanthropy; it is also where power asymmetries are most visible — the funder has structural power over the grantee, and program officers vary substantially in how they navigate this.

**Field development.** Program officers increasingly function as field strategists, not just grant managers. This involves convening grantees, facilitating coordination across organizations working on the same issues, connecting grantees to complementary funders, and synthesizing field intelligence back to foundation leadership. The best program officers are as useful to their grantees as intermediaries and field strategists as they are as grantmakers.

**Internal advocacy.** Program officers spend substantial time managing upward — advocating to program directors and boards for specific grants and strategies. The internal political skill of a program officer (ability to frame proposals compellingly to board members who may be conservative on political risk) is often more consequential than the external portfolio decisions.

## Intermediaries: when they outperform direct foundation grantmaking

Philanthropic intermediaries (fiscal sponsors, donor-advised funds, and regranting organizations) play an increasingly important role in the progressive funding ecosystem, particularly for smaller and more radical organizations that don't fit the profile of typical foundation grantees.

**Fiscal sponsors** (Tides Foundation, NEO Philanthropy, Borealis Philanthropy, Movement Voter Project, Emergent Fund) provide legal and financial infrastructure for organizations that don't have their own 501(c)(3) status. They receive grants from foundations and pass them through to project organizations, managing compliance, payroll, and legal structure. For grassroots and movement organizations that are too small, too new, or too radical to receive direct foundation grants, fiscal sponsorship is often the only path to institutional funding.

**Regranting intermediaries** (Wellspring Philanthropic Fund, NEO Democracy Fund, Borealis) aggregate funds from multiple donors and make grants that individual donors couldn't sustain independently. The intermediary provides the diversification logic: a donor who wants to support progressive advocacy without directly managing a portfolio can give to NEO or Wellspring and rely on their staff to make the grant decisions. Intermediaries also provide political cover — a foundation that funds Wellspring, rather than directly funding a campaign organization, has more deniability around controversial grantees.

The argument for intermediaries: they reduce transaction costs for small grantees, provide access to funding for organizations that can't manage foundation compliance requirements, and can move faster than traditional foundation processes. The argument against: they add a layer of overhead and bureaucracy between donor intent and grantee use; they create their own power asymmetries with fiscal-sponsored organizations; and they can become ineffective or captured by their own institutional interests.

For careers in philanthropy, intermediary roles offer a genuine alternative to foundation program-officer positions. Program staff at Borealis, NEO, or Tides manage grant portfolios, work closely with grantees, and often have more flexibility to fund grassroots and advocacy work than staff at traditional foundations. Salaries are comparable to foundation program-officer roles.

## Donor organizing: Patriotic Millionaires and Resource Generation

Two organizations at the intersection of philanthropy and advocacy deserve specific attention because they represent unusual career models.

**Patriotic Millionaires** (covered also in [Advocacy and Campaign Organizations](/organizations/advocacy-campaigns/)) organizes high-net-worth individuals to publicly advocate for higher taxes on wealth. Staff roles include member recruitment and engagement, event and campaign logistics, policy research and communications, and government relations. Unlike most advocacy organizations, Patriotic Millionaires' staff work primarily on *mobilizing and managing the members* rather than doing direct advocacy themselves. The skill profile is unusual: it requires comfort with high-net-worth individuals, the ability to manage and coordinate a volunteer membership, and the policy expertise to brief members on the issues they're advocating for.

**Resource Generation** organizes young people (under 35) with wealth or the prospect of inheriting wealth toward more progressive political giving. It is explicitly a donor-education and political-organizing organization: it provides community, political analysis, and peer pressure for wealthy young people to give more and give more strategically to progressive causes. Staff roles include organizing (managing member chapters), education programming, and communications. Resource Generation is unusual in the progressive space in that it explicitly engages with the psychology of privilege and the transformation of donor identity — it's closer to organizing than to grantmaking.

Both organizations represent a model of **elite mobilization** that is analytically distinct from direct advocacy or from traditional philanthropy. The career path they create is unusual: professionals who understand how to move high-net-worth individuals politically and philanthropically are scarce and in demand across the progressive sector.

## Family-office and HNW-advisor roles

For finance and accounting professionals interested in proximity to this field without entering the foundation world directly, family offices are an underexplored option.

Major family offices with significant policy or philanthropic programs include: Emerson Collective (Laurene Powell Jobs' vehicle; large investments in immigration, education, and climate); the Audacious Project (coordinated large-scale philanthropy); and numerous smaller single-family offices that manage the wealth and philanthropic programs of HNW individuals aligned with this agenda.

**Mission-related investing** (MRI) is a growing field within family offices and larger foundations in which endowment assets are invested in ways consistent with the foundation's mission — not just in market-rate assets to maximize returns for grantmaking. The Heron Foundation was an early leader in MRI; MacArthur, Ford, and Kresge have large MRI programs. These programs employ investment professionals with both finance skills and mission literacy, and they represent an unusual career bridge between finance and the social-sector world.

**Program-related investments** (PRIs) are a specific [IRS-sanctioned tool](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/private-foundations/taxes-on-failure-to-distribute-income-private-foundations) that allows foundations to make below-market-rate investments that advance their charitable purpose, counting the investment against their [5% annual distribution requirement](https://www.philanthropyroundtable.org/resource/private-foundations-and-the-5-percent-payout-rule/). Community development financial institutions (CDFIs) frequently receive PRIs from foundations; the management of PRI portfolios requires both financial analysis (loan underwriting, deal structuring) and programmatic assessment skills. CPA and finance backgrounds are genuinely differentiating in these roles.

## The structural critique of philanthropy and how to navigate it

The structural critique is real and worth engaging with directly: the large foundations active in inequality work are endowed with wealth generated by the same economic structures they now fund work to reform. The Rockefeller Foundation was built on oil monopoly proceeds; Ford on automobile manufacturing; Hewlett on technology industry wealth. More recent entrants are built on financial-sector wealth (Open Society) or tech-sector wealth (Omidyar, Arnold).

This creates predictable blind spots: foundations systematically underfund labor organizing (which challenges the employers of their endowment's stock holdings), undervalue general operating support relative to project grants, and overweight technocratic interventions that don't require fundamental power redistribution. The evidence on this is well-documented in Edgar Villanueva's [*Decolonizing Wealth*](https://www.bkconnection.com/books/title/Decolonizing-Wealth-Second-Edition) (Berrett-Koehler, 2018; second edition 2021) and in academic research on foundation strategy and grantee satisfaction.

The most effective program officers acknowledge this structural position and counterweight it in their portfolio decisions:
- Prioritizing **general operating support** (unrestricted funding that organizations can use however needed) over restricted project grants.
- Funding **grassroots organizations** that don't have the grant-writing infrastructure to compete for conventional foundation funding.
- Supporting **organizing and base-building** rather than only research and advocacy.
- Maintaining **transparency with grantees** about the power dynamics of the funder-grantee relationship.
- Advocating **internally** for strategic directions that the board might find uncomfortable.

This internal advocacy role is one of the most important and least visible contributions a program officer can make. The leverage point is that foundation boards and CEOs are often more risk-averse than the organizations in the field; a program officer who can translate field intelligence into compelling internal recommendations shapes foundation strategy in ways that are invisible from outside.

## Compensation and culture

Foundation program-officer compensation is among the highest in the social sector. The funding rationale is that foundations need to compete with private sector for talent capable of managing complex multimillion-dollar portfolios.

| Role / level                        | Approximate range (USD)    | Notes                                            |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Program associate / grants manager  | $65,000–$90,000            | Entry-level; foundation admin tracks             |
| Program officer                     | $100,000–$160,000          | Core grantmaking role; varies by foundation size |
| Senior program officer              | $130,000–$200,000          | Portfolio leadership; board exposure             |
| Program director                    | $160,000–$250,000          | Manages a team of program officers              |
| Vice president / senior director    | $200,000–$350,000          | Reported on 990s for large foundations           |
| Intermediary staff (program officer) | $80,000–$130,000          | Slightly below major-foundation equivalents      |
| Family-office program staff         | Wide range ($90K–$200K+)   | Highly variable by office size and wealth level  |

Sources: Foundation program-officer ranges cross-checked against [ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer 990 filings](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/) (VP/director compensation is reported for large foundations); intermediary ranges reflect comparable-size organization filings for Borealis, NEO Philanthropy, and Tides; family-office ranges are highly variable and drawn from sector surveys.

Foundation benefits tend to be excellent: strong health coverage, defined-contribution retirement with substantial employer match, and generous professional development budgets. The culture is typically less intense than advocacy or legal roles — foundations are often described (accurately) as calmer environments than the organizations they fund.

The cultural disadvantage of foundation work is its distance from impact. Program officers move large amounts of money but the actual outcomes are achieved by the organizations they fund. For candidates who want direct operational responsibility for advocacy or research outcomes, the intermediary distance is a real frustration; for candidates who prefer strategic influence to operational execution, it is an advantage.
